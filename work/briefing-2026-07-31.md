# Briefing — what changed since the April update

**Prepared 2026-07-31. Site is live and current: https://mjbommar.github.io/moratorium-data-2026/**

## The one-line version

The moratorium count nearly doubled, from 222 to **416** — but almost all of that
is us looking harder at the same period, not a sudden surge. The genuinely new
signal is that **170 instruments were adopted in May–July alone**, June was the
single biggest month on record, and the fight has moved from rural townships into
**major cities**.

## The five things most likely to come up

### 1. Big cities are now doing this, not just townships

This is the real change in character since April. Newly adopted:

- **Seattle** — emergency moratorium, Ordinance 127447, 2026-06-09
- **Cleveland** — Ordinance 556-2026, passed 14–1 on 2026-07-15 (cut from
  12 months to 3 in committee)
- **Minneapolis** — six-month interim ordinance, adopted 8–5, effective 2026-06-25
- **Denver** — Council Bill 26-0431, one year, effective 2026-05-21
- **Charlotte** — 150 days, 2026-06-08
- **Durham** — city *and* county each adopted their own, separately
- **Montgomery County, MD** — 18-month pause plus a hyperscale ban, 2026-07-28
- **Lansing, Flint** (MI), **Baltimore City** (pending)

### 2. "Temporary" is proving to be a euphemism

- **DeKalb County, GA** voted its permanent ordinance **down** on 2026-06-23, then
  extended the moratorium to **2027-03-30**. That's the clearest example of a
  pause becoming the policy.
- **Prince George's County, MD** re-upped for **two more years** (CR-066-2026)
  rather than let it resolve into permanent rules.
- **Larimer County, CO** extended a second time, six months past what we had.
- Of 50 status changes on existing rows, **15 were extensions** — the most common
  outcome by far.

### 3. But some do resolve — and a few just lapse

- **Converted to permanent rules (9):** Griffin GA, Polk County GA, Troup County
  GA, St. Charles Parish LA, Montour County PA, Brevard NC, Jonesborough TN,
  Peculiar MO, St. Charles MO.
- **Lapsed with nothing adopted (6):** La Grange KY, Waterville OH, Shutesbury MA,
  Dundee Township MI, Howell Township MI.
- **Repealed early (1):** Oliver County ND, seven weeks in, reportedly right after
  a data-center zoning application was filed.
- **Voted down:** Lowell Township MI rejected a moratorium 2–5 despite a packed
  hearing, letting a Microsoft-linked project proceed. Hill County TX rescinded
  after a **$100M developer lawsuit**.

### 4. Legislatures acted — 38 bills enacted in 2026

The center of gravity is **ratepayer protection**, not bans:

- **New Jersey A796/S731** — signed **2026-07-07**, three weeks ago. Large-load
  data center tariffs. The most recent major enactment.
- **Oklahoma HB 2992** — Data Center Customer Protection Act, 2026-05-11.
- **Maryland Utility RELIEF Act** (HB1532/SB0841) — 2026-05-12.
- **Idaho H0911** — PUC-approved contracts for any new 50 MW+ load, a "no harm
  test," and stranded-cost security. Quietly one of the strongest in the country.
- **Nebraska LB1010** — Large Load Customer Regulation Act.
- **Tennessee HB1847/SB2128** — infrastructure cost obligations at 50 MW+.
- **Virginia** — four bills signed 2026-04-13 (sound-profile site assessments,
  water reporting).
- **Florida CS/CS/SB 484** — notable for cutting the other way: it *preserves*
  local land-use authority over large-load customers.

Counter-trend worth knowing: **Arizona and Illinois both paused their data-center
tax incentives** rather than permitting. Those are not moratoria and we
deliberately exclude them, but expect them to be described as such.

### 5. Sector spread

Most instruments name data centers, but a large share also cover
**cryptocurrency mining**, and a growing number bundle **battery storage, solar,
and wind**. Shelby County, Iowa adopted four separate resolutions in one meeting —
one per sector. If asked whether this is "anti-renewables," the honest answer is
that most of these are data-center-driven and sweep in other uses because the
zoning gap is the same.

## What NOT to claim

**Do not say the number of moratoria doubled in three months.** It didn't. The
count went from 222 to 416 mostly because we swept systematically for the first
time. Of the 194 added rows, 170 were adopted in the May–July window — the rest
are older instruments we simply hadn't found.

**416 is a floor, not a count.** We swept 15 states month-by-month; the other 35
got only opportunistic coverage. Florida went from 1 row to 14 purely from being
searched — it did not change in the world. Expect the real national number to be
meaningfully higher.

**Five swept states genuinely found nothing** (Alaska, Arizona, Delaware, Hawaii,
Idaho at the local level). That's a real finding, not a gap — worth distinguishing
if someone asks why a state is missing.

**The clause-level analysis is older than the inventory.** The 44-clause taxonomy
covers 348 documents across 211 jurisdictions collected before April. Fine to cite
for *what moratoria contain*; do not present it as describing all 416.

Roughly a quarter of rows still carry a `[VERIFY]` flag — mostly small
jurisdictions that don't post signed ordinances. We say so publicly.

## If they ask "what should we do about it"

The dataset is descriptive and doesn't take a position. What it does show:

- The regulatory gap is the trigger. Most of these ordinances say plainly that the
  zoning code has no definition for the use.
- Jurisdictions that drafted permanent rules **before** the pause expired resolved
  cleanly. Those that didn't tended to extend, repeatedly.
- The state legislative response has converged on cost allocation — who pays for
  the interconnection and the capacity — more than on siting bans.
