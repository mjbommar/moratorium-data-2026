PLANNING AND ZONING COMMISSION

Glenville Environmental Conservation Commission (GECC) Presentation on Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS)


Dan Hill, Chairman of the GECC, was present.

D. Hill asked the commission how familiar they are with the concepts/operation of battery

systems. The members indicated they had some knowledge.

D. Hill said when the term battery storage is used it is not a large warehouse to store batteries. It

means using batteries for energy storage. These types of batteries are rechargeable just like in

cell phones, laptops, and hybrid cars. They can be scaled up to provide more power by

combining a bunch of them. When they are combined in bunches they are formally referred to as

Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS).

At the small end of the scale, a small bunch can be a unit for a house and serve the same purpose

as a generator for power outages. A little bigger system can be a backup or supplement for

businesses. They can be scaled way up to the megawatt capacity size whereas a battery farm they

look like a parking area with tracker trailers all lined up next to each other.

At any scale, the battery systems are designed to be interconnected with the electrical grid to

help level out and compensate for energy supply/demand and fluctuations and to steady the

fluctuations in pricing that goes along with that. They charge up and store energy when the

supply is higher than demand and discharge it to the building/site/grid when the demand

requires. They are a power reservoir.

Energy storage of different technologies has been around for a long time. Battery systems have

been around for a long time but battery farms have become more common in the last few years.

The recharge technology has reached a point where they become an economically attractive land

use opportunity. Sometimes they are co-located on sites where electricity is generated by wind or

solar but it is a mischaracterization to think they are only associated with generating sites or that

they are only proliferating because of the rules. They can be stand alone facilities. Whatever

power source gas, wind, solar, or nuclear hydro. The batteries do not care what makes the

electrons move. Regardless of the source, adding storage capability to the grid is now more

feasible and seen as a way to improve stability, reliability, efficiency, and economy. That is why

these are becoming more common.

There are not any proposed for Glenville and at the same time Glenville does not have any

specific regulations for this type of use. There is already a relatively small facility in Glenville

that was sited by the County through general regulations. As a result, the GECC suggested to the

Town Board that a moratorium be enacted on any more facilities to allow time to develop

specific regulations to be prepared if a project comes along. The board did pass a moratorium

which goes until the end of July 2026. The supervisor asked the GECC to get the ball rolling

with drafting recommendations. The version presented tonight is still a work in progress, but

they think it is to the point where it is organized and detailed enough that it would be worth the

PZC’s time to review it. There are also areas where there are questions that could use the

knowledge/p/perspective of the PZC members to answer.

In getting to this stage, they looked at materials from the state agency of Energy and Research

Development for municipalities that covers the technology itself and safety considerations, a

model zoning law from that agency, and the final law that Rotterdam enacted last year. They

checked with the state and Rotterdam about their rationale for their versions. A Additionally, they

researched technical and legal info from government websites, scientific organizations, industry

sites, and news sites. The GECC visited the county’s 2.5 megawatt site off Route 5. The

commission used experience from reviewing solar farm projects under the town’s solar farm

zoning law and parts of that made sense to parallel. They saw ways to improve on some

ambiguities in applying that law. There was other miscellaneous research done as well. They

went back to the state energy agency regarding iterations of their draft. All through this there

were discussions amongst the committee members, Planning Department, and Town Board. They

drew from the combined information and worked to balance that all out towards an end resulting

in a format and content for the town board to actually consider passing it into law. The objective

was to keep the content understandable to anyone who has not been working on this. Their hopes

are that the draft is, although long, is clear.

K. Semon asked if the tiers of batteries are limited to the type of battery, i.e., lithium, lithium ion,

They are recommending a new article be added to town code to specifically address battery

systems similar to what was done for solar farms. In that article, they are defining three different

levels of battery systems; residential, commercial, and utility scale. Each of those has increasing

requirements of permitting and operational levels as they go up. The scale is based upon

electrical storage capacity in kilowatt hours. A  A residential would be the size of a half a

refrigerator, commercial would be from half a refrigerator to 3 refrigerators, and utility is

anything larger than 3 refrigerators. All would have to comply with the building, electrical and

fire codes. The state expanded the fire code for more safety requirements (battery systems) based

on investigations of battery facilities fire causes within the past couple of years.

Utility scale systems should be restricted from areas like floodplains/wetlands, aquifer recharge

zones and designated open spaces under the town’s open space plan. For locations outside of

those areas, utility scale would first require the establishment of an overlay zone by the town

board which is similar to the solar farm law. For the zoning change application, the developer has

plans that the applicant has to provide per the NYSERDA model law and fire code. For the site

plan application, GECC takes the first look and then Planning & Zoning completes full review

(that would be similar to how you handle site plan reviews for other projects) with some

For residential use, they are looking at battery units as similar to other electrical appliances

Building Department but not review by GECC, PZC or the Town Board. For commercial scale

systems, that is the main area they still have questions on how they should be treated,. It is up for

discussion with the PZC.

Under state law, local jurisdiction applies to stand alone battery systems under 80 megawatts

while 80 megawatts and over is state jurisdiction however, local approvals may factor in. If the

battery site is combined with a renewable energy source the threshold goes down. Local

jurisdiction is up to but not including 25 megawatts while 25 and above is state jurisdiction.
