The joint public hearing relates to a package of zoning amendments to incorporate mandatory standards for

consolidated local permitting of Small Clean Energy Infrastructure Facilities (SCEIF) in compliance with 225

CMR 29.00, Chair Tait advised.

At the earlier hearing, members raised questions with regard to energy storage systems; specifically, whether

the maximum threshold of 500kWh for Tier 1 is too large, given that they are proposed to be allowed in

residential districts without site plan review, Director Misch reminded. Generally, the larger the tier, the smaller

the geographic locations in which they would be allowed, she stressed. As part of the discussion, members

asked about the permitting process; i.e. –  – “even if it’s allowed by right; who reviews it?” Finally, there was a

request for feedback from city officials familiar with the systems from consumer and emergency response

perspectives.

Director Misch met with Fire Chief Andy Pelis and Climate Action and Project Administration (CAPA) Director

Ben Weil and reviewed the current tiered definition as well as a model bylaw developed by the Energy Facilities

Siting Board (EFSB), she reported. The model bylaw reduces the amount of energy storage in respective tiers,

lowering the maximum kilowatt hour (kWh) of storage in Tier 1 to 250 kWh and in Tier II to 10 megawatt-hours

(MWh).

As a result of their discussions, Director Misch reviewed an updated tiered definition of storage as follows:

Tier 1 –  – up to 250 kWh of storage

Tier 2 –  – 250 kWh to 10 MWh

Tier 3 –  – 10 MWh to 100 MWh

“They were very comfortable with that,” she asserted. 250 kWh would accommodate multi-family battery

storage, she pointed out.

With respect to how National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) codes interact with zoning regulations, when

an applicant applies to the building department to install a battery storage system, the fire department reviews

the application for compliance with NFPA standards, Director Misch explained. For single-family homes, the

NFPA currently caps aggregate battery storage capacity at 80 kWh. [This may have been the genesis for the

sentiment that 500 kWh is too high for residential, she suggested.] She voiced her expectation that, at some

point, the NFPA will catch up and expand this limit. Meanwhile, by having a larger allowance, their zoning would

allow for future NFPA modifications and acknowledge the presence of multi-family units such as condominium

associations or apartment complexes which might have demand for greater storage capacity.

What’s before them is a recommended adjustment, she confirmed. Otherwise, with regard to submittal and

review criteria, the fire chief and climate director still feel very comfortable with the standards proposed. They

acknowledge that technology is changing rapidly to include chemical compositions much less likely to ignite and

be a problem, she added.

Councilor Maiore asked about safeguards for private wells.

The language of the text includes the requirement to show, procedurally, how the system is being protected and

how it is designed to minimize impacts, Director Misch advised. She thinks the issue would be more w with larger

systems, which wouldn’t be allowed in areas of the city where there are wells.

2

Councilor Stratton said he was really pleased last week with points made by Planning Board member Baker and

Chair Tait about the size of home energy systems that would fall under Tier I. For a major manufacturer [Tesla],

Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) generally come in increments of 13/14 kWh. He thought both made a

really good argument for the 100kWh threshold. He understands the multi-family case; however, he is looking at

it as the difference between an administrative permit and a special permit. Unfortunately, building permits

effectively became secret this spring, he noted. They used to be visible on line with all the details, but they

vanished, so, while users can now see that a permit was issued, they are unable to see what it was for.

Theoretically, it could be obtained with a public records request, but that would really be a fishing expedition.

“Whatever we put in Tier 1, we’re basically saying that your neighbors are going to have no knowledge that this

is happening,” he reflected.

Promising new, very low-risk battery technologies are on the horizon, he acknowledged, and, as chemistries get

safer, they could consider liberalizing things. For now, he would like to see some thought put into the benefit of

requiring a special permit which involves a public process vs. only an administrative permit.

Director Misch clarified that the oversight requirement is for site plan review, not a special permit.

Member Tait said that, to him, installing batteries in a house is equivalent to putting solar panels on a roof, so he

doesn’t see the need for building permits to be publicly available. Instead of zoning, he pointed to the Fire

Chief’s review of applications for compliance with NFPA standards as a better protection from possible risk.

Member Baker commented that, after the last meeting, given his experience of having a 13.5 kWh battery in his

home, he was pretty convinced that 100 kWh was sufficient for Tier 1. A larger family might have three, but that

would still be under 50 kWh, he noted. “It still seems awfully generous,” he observed.

With regard to Councilor Stratton’s comment about permit applications no longer being visible, Councilor Perry

suggested it might be part of a streamlining effort undertaken by Central Services to change platforms to make

information more accessible.

Chair Tait pointed out that the allowance for Tier 1 energy storage capacity is for all residential zones and so

applies to more than just single-family homes. In response, Member Baker clarified that it wasn’t necessarily

clear to him that 250 is not reasonable.

“Technology is changing so rapidly and uses are changing so rapidly,” he acknowledged. As Councilor Stratton

pointed out, they can modify these things as times change. He thinks for today it seems awfully generous.

Councilor Stratton wondered if they could say 100 by right, 250 by site plan review. He thought a good

argument was made that 100 is more than sufficient for a single-family or a duplex.

Councilor Loisel said her understanding is that these are not just for single-family homes but for apartment

buildings and that the Fire Chief feels comfortable with 250 kWh. It could be for a school or a church or other

things in residential zones, Chair Tait added.

Councilor Loisel said she thinks simplicity is important. This seems reasonable to her.

For himself, Member Tait said he didn’t understand at the last hearing that an NFPA standard also applied and

so thought that whatever zoning definition was approved alone set the hard limit. He feels comfortable with the

NFPA standard of 80 in place for single-family homes, he volunteered.

Councilor Maiore asked about Councilor Stratton’s comfort level with moving forward without further dividing the

Councilor Stratton said he couldn’t really say he’s comfortable with it but expressed his understanding that this

Members discussed whether they had enough information to close the hearing. Member Kohout shared his

Councilor Stratton commented that a decommissioning bond for Tier 2 and Tier 3 systems makes sense since

Again, Chair Tait compared a residential battery system to solar panels, saying he doesn’t know that it is

26.125

AN ORDINANCE AMENDING THE NORTHAMPTON ZONING CODE TO IMPOSE A

TEMPORARY MORATORIUM ON DATA CENTERS

WHEREAS, with the proliferation of Data Center expansion in Massachusetts, cities and towns

have adopted moratoriums to allow time to review their zoning and land use, and to formulate

regulations governing the impacts of these facilities on the City and larger community and

environment; and

WHEREAS, Data Centers raise concerns about overuse of resources such as water, electricity

and land, as well as about noise and lighting of such facilities;

WHEREAS, the City of Northampton’s zoning, land use and development regulations currently

make no specific provision for Data Centers within the City, either by right or special use permit;

and

WHEREAS, a Council Select Committee consisting of City elected officials, City Staff and City

residents will be tasked to review the impacts that the construction, expansion, and operation of

commercial Data Centers may have on City infrastructure, the environment and the overall

quality of life for the residents of Northampton and will make regulatory recommendations; and

WHEREAS, a temporary moratorium will provide the Select Committee the time needed to

study those impacts and issue regulatory and mitigation recommendations to the City Council

and the Mayor.

THEREFORE BE IT ORDAINED by the City Council of Northampton, a moratorium is imposed,

to remain in effect for 365 days from its passage in City Council, on any new construction and

development within the City of Northampton of “Data Centers”, as defined. "Chapter 350 entitled

"Zoning” of Ordinances of the City of Northampton, Massachusetts", shall be amended, as

follows:

1. Amend Article 350-2.1, “Definitions”, by inserting a new definition and classification as

follows:

DATA CENTER:

A facility, or portion thereof, primarily designed or used to house computer servers and related

information technology infrastructure for the storage, processing, management, or transmission

of electronic data. A Data Center may include, but is not limited to, server racks, data storage

systems, cooling systems, power conditioning equipment, substations, uninterruptible power

supplies, backup generators, telecommunications equipment, security systems, and associated

mechanical and electrical infrastructure. For purposes of this ordinance, a Data Center shall not

be considered an Office, Warehouse, Manufacturing, or Research use.

2. Amend Article 350-5.2, “Table of Use Regulations”, by inserting a new Section as

follows:

5-2.D. 365-DAY MORATORIUM ON DATA CENTERS

1. Moratorium. The permitting, construction, conversion, or establishment of any Data

Center in the City shall be prohibited for 365 days from the date of the passage of this

ordinance, unless earlier terminated in accordance with subsection 6, below..

2. No applications. During the moratorium, no application, permit, special permit, site plan

approval, variance, building permit, zoning permit, or other land use approval shall be

approved for a Data Center.

3. Pending applications. Any pending application for a Data Center shall be stayed during

the moratorium.

4. Review. During the moratorium, the Joint Committee shall review environmental, public

health, and infrastructure impacts to better inform permanent zoning regulations.

5. Extension. The moratorium may be extended to 180 days, once, upon request of the

Joint Committee and majority vote of approval of the City Council.

6. Termination. If the City adopts permanent zoning regulations governing Data Centers

before expiration of the moratorium, this Section shall terminate on the effective date of

those regulations.

7. Severability. If any provision of this Ordinance is held invalid, the remaining provisions

shall remain in full force and effect.
