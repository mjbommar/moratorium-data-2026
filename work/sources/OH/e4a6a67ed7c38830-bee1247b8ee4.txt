I. FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

{¶ 3} Bristolville acquires and develops real property for construction of

large-scale data centers throughout the United States. In February 2025,

Bristolville agreed to purchase from BHGH Properties a parcel of real property

located in Lordstown on which Bristolville seeks to construct a data center. The

property is zoned for industrial use.

A. Site-Plan Review under the Lordstown Ordinance and Overview Process

{¶ 4} Before an applicant may request a zoning permit for new construction

in Lordstown, local law requires projects to undergo a “Site Plan Review” overseen

by Lordstown’s Planning and Zoning Office. Chapter 1115 of the Lordstown

Codified Ordinances sets forth the site-plan-review process. Subject to exceptions

2

not applicable here, “[n]o permit for the construction . . . of any building shall be

given . . . except in conformity with a site plan approved by the Planning

Commission.” Lordstown Cod.Ord. 1115.02, available at Village of Lordstown,

Codified Ordinances of Lordstown, OH,H,

https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/lordstown/latest/lordstown_oh/0-0-0-15997

(accessed Sept. 17, 2026) [https://perma.cc/2SVE-YFYX].

{¶ 5} To explain the preliminary steps to submitting a site plan for approval

by the Planning Commission, the Lordstown Planning and Zoning Office provides

to prospective zoning-permit applicants a two-page document titled “Overview of

Lordstown Village Site Plan Review Process” (“the Overview”). The Overview

details four steps in the process.

{¶ 6} Under “step one,” a zoning-permit applicant must submit to the

Planning and Zoning Office drafts of a narrative outline of the project and a

“site/plot plan identifying property size, setbacks from all property lines to the

proposed facility . . . , and other project details.” Once the applicant submits these

draft materials, “the Village of Lordstown will begin the process of engaging the

Village Engineer(s) on the project.” The village engineer will then prepare and

provide to the developer a “work authorization,” which the developer must sign and

return to Lordstown as an acknowledgment of the developer’s responsibility to

reimburse Lordstown for the village engineer’s fees related to the project. When

the signed work acknowledgment is received, the village council will approve the

village engineer’s engagement on the project.

{¶ 7} Once the village council approves the village engineer’s engagement

on the project, the Overview process moves on to “step two,” the “pre-application

meeting” required by Lordstown Cod.Ord. 1115.04(a). Under step two, before the

pre-application meeting, the developer must submit detailed project information,

including a preliminary-planning-evaluation form, a site-plan-review form, utility

plans, a storm-water-management plan, hazard/safety plans, and approvals from

3

other regulatory entities. The Overview contemplates that after submission of these

draft materials, a pre-application meeting will occur and should include “everyone

from the Village team & everyone from the Developer’s team.”

{¶ 8} Following the pre-application meeting, “step three” of the Overview

contemplates that the village engineer and planning-and-zoning administrator will

each provide written comments on the proposed site plan, identifying deficiencies

or additional information that is needed. Other Lordstown department heads may

also provide feedback for the developer to address. The developer is required in

step three to address all comments, questions, and concerns “by providing revised

drafts, responses, & updates.”

{¶ 9} Once the site-plan details have been fully addressed and the draft site

plan documents updated, the Overview process proceeds to final submission of the

site plan under ““step f fofour.”  Final site-plan documents are submitted to L Lordstown’s

planning-and-zoning administrator and must “address all revisions, questions,

concerns or comments from the pre-application meetings & any individual

department discussions/meetings.” The applicant must file a copy of the final site

plan with the planning-and-zoning administrator for approval by the Planning

Commission. Lordstown Cod.Ord. 1115.04(a).

{¶ 10} Upon the developer’s filing of the final site plan, the Planning

Commission “shall review the site plan pursuant to the site plan review guidelines

set forth in [Lordstown Cod.Ord.] 1115.05.” Lordstown Cod.Ord. 1115.04(c). If

the Planning Commission approves the final submission, the applicant may file an

application for a zoning permit for the Planning and Zoning Office’s approval. Id.

No construction may begin until the zoning permit has been issued. Lordstown

Cod.Ord. 1115.02.

II. EVIDENTIARY MOTIONS

A. Motion for Leave to File Rebuttal Evidence

{¶ 18} Relators filed a timely motion for leave to file rebuttal evidence

under S.Ct.Prac.R. 12.06(B), asking to file proposed Exhibits JJ through OO along

with the authenticating affidavit of their counsel. Relators state that the proposed

exhibits consist entirely of public records and that most were obtained from

Lordstown through a public-records request. The village opposes the motion.

7

{¶ 19} Relators’ proposed rebuttal evidence consists of documents relating

to the site-plan review and zoning-permit application to construct industrial

structures on property owned and developed by a nonparty to this case. According

to relators, this rebuttal evidence is relevant in this case to respond to the village’s

arguments that Bristolville has not yet applied for a zoning permit and that the

Overview is but “an informal aid and roadmap” for the site-plan-review process.

The rebuttal evidence relating to the site-plan review and zoning application of

another developer for a different project, say relators, demonstrate that the village

follows the Overview generally but refuses to do so with respect to Bristolville’s

project.

{¶ 20} Rebuttal evidence “is that given to explain, refute, or disprove new

facts introduced into evidence by the adverse party; it becomes relevant only to

challenge the evidence offered by the opponent, and its scope is limited by such

evidence.” State v. McNeill, 1998-Ohio-293, ¶ 44. We have discretion in

determining whether to allow rebuttal evidence. Id.

{¶ 21} We deny relators’ motion. The village has argued in its merit brief

that Lordstown Cod.Ord. Ch. 1115 governs the site-plan review and zoning-permit

process. And while the Overview is an informal aid for the village and developers,

the village noted, the Overview “does not take the place of the Village’s

ordinances.” Whether the village followed the Overview with respect to the site

plan review and zoning-permit applications of a third party does not refute the

village’s position that the ordinances—n—not the Overview—set forth the legal duty

enforceable in mandamus. See State ex rel. Shie v. Ohio Adult Parole Auth., 2022-

Ohio-270, ¶ 11 (“An internal policy of an agency does not create a legal duty

enforceable in mandamus”).

{¶ 22} Relators’ argument that the proposed rebuttal evidence responds to

the village’s argument that Bristolville has not yet applied for a zoning permit also

lacks merit. Under Lordstown Cod.Ord. 11154.04(c), an application for a permit

8

may not be submitted until the site-plan review has been completed. Relators’

proposed rebuttal evidence does not refute the village’s contention in this regard.

If anything, the proposed evidence corroborates the village’s position; that is, it

demonstrates that a third party completed the site-plan process before submitting

its application for a zoning permit.

{¶ 23} For these reasons, we deny relators’ motion for leave to file rebuttal

evidence.

III. ANALYSIS

{¶ 27} To be entitled to a writ of mandamus, relators must establish by clear

and convincing evidence a clear legal right to the relief they requested, a clear legal

duty on the part of the village to provide that relief, and the lack of an adequate

remedy in the ordinary course of the law. State ex rel. A.N. v. Cuyahoga Cty.

Prosecutor’s ’s Office, 2021-Ohio-2071, ¶ 9.

{¶ 28} In attempting to carry their burden to show a clear legal right and

duty enforceable in mandamus, relators rely on Lordstown Cod.Ord. Ch. 1115,

which sets forth the process that developers and Lordstown officials must follow

for the submission, review, and—d—u—ultimately—approval of a site plan for new

construction in L Lordstown. In addition, relators ask that the village perform its site

plan-review duties “under [Lordstown]’s applicable laws in effect as of October 20,

2025,” the date on which relators submitted their draft site-plan-review materials.

In their merit brief, relators clarify that they ask for the site-plan review to be

conducted under Lordstown’s applicable laws in effect before the village council

enacted the data-center moratorium.

10

A. The Village’s Threshold Arguments

1. D Declaratory Judgment and Prohibitory Injunction

{¶ 29} The village argues that relators’ mandamus action is a disguised civil

action for a declaratory judgment, over which this court lacks original jurisdiction.

The village contends that relators’ true object is not a writ of mandamus but, rather,

a declaratory judgment that the village’s action on Bristolville’s site-plan review

will be governed by local law in effect on October 20, 2025, the date on which

Bristolville submitted its draft site-plan documents. To be sure, relators have done

little to disguise their desire t that we declare that they have a vested right to proceed

with the site-plan process based on the law as it existed on October 20, 2025, before

the village council passed the moratorium on data centers. Nonetheless, we reject

the village’s argument.

{¶ 30} The fact that a mandamus complaint contains a declaratory

judgment component is not fatal. “What distinguishes a proper mandamus

complaint from an improper one is not whether the relator is seeking declaratory

judgment as part of the complaint but whether the complaint seeks to prevent or

compel official action.” (Emphasis in original.) State ex rel. Ethics First–t–You

Decide Ohio Political Action Commt. v. DeWine, 2016-Ohio-3144, ¶ 10. In this

case, relators are asking us to compel the village to comply with its alleged duty to

implement and abide by L Lordstown’s site-plan-review process and to move forward

with Bristolville’s site-plan submission. This is true even though relators are

demanding that the site-plan submission be reviewed under Lordstown’s applicable

laws in effect as of October 20, 2025 (when Bristolville submitted its draft site-plan

documents) or, at the latest, as of January 4, 2026 (before the data-center

moratorium went into effect). Even though the requested relief would, in effect,

prohibit the enforcement of the moratorium, such a circumstance does not change

the character of the relief relators requested. “The fact that adjudicating [a] case

requires [a] court also to prohibit [an] official from acting under the current version

11

of [a law] is ‘only ancillary’ and does not alter the fundamental nature of the relief

sought.” Id. at ¶ 11, quoting State ex rel. Zupancic v. Limbach, 58 Ohio St.3d 130,

133 (1991); s see also State ex rel. Pike Cty. Convention & Visitor’s Bur. v. Pike Cty.

Bd. of Commrs., 2021-Ohio-4031, ¶ 14 (holding that the relator stated a valid

mandamus claim for performance of an official act “even though the requested

relief would, in effect, prohibit the enforcement of . . . more recent county

resolutions”).

{¶ 31} We therefore reject the village’s contention that we lack subject

matter j jurisdiction over relators’ action.

2. This Action Is Not Moot

{¶ 32} The village also argues that this case is moot, emphasizing the

language of relators’ prayer for relief in their complaint. In the complaint, relators

request a writ of mandamus “directing Respondents . . . to follow Lordstown

Codified Ordinance 1115 by accepting, reviewing, and acting upon Bristolville’s

Submission and to do so throughout the Site Plan Review Process under the

Village’s applicable laws in effect . . . when no data center ban was in effect in the

Village.” (Emphasis added.) Because the data-center ban that relators’ complaint

refers to was repealed, the village argues, the mandamus action is moot. And even

though a moratorium on data centers is in effect, the village contends, the

moratorium does not inform the issue whether the village will ultimately grant or

deny a permit for Bristolville’s project.

{¶ 33} The repeal of the data-center ban is not a proper basis for dismissing

this case as moot. The gravamen of relators’ action is that the village is not moving

forward with the site-plan-review process as set forth in Lordstown Cod.Ord. Ch.

1115. Indeed, r relators’ r requested relief is no different now than b before the ban was

repealed: they seek a writ of mandamus compelling the village to perform the duties

set forth in the Lordstown Codified Ordinances for reviewing and acting on

12

Bristolville’s site-plan submission. The repeal of the data-center ban does not moot

relators’ pursuit of this relief.

3. The Requested Act Has Not Been Performed

{¶ 34} The village next argues that the writ should be denied because the

village is already performing the act that relators are asking this court to compel

through a writ of mandamus. A writ of mandamus will not issue to compel an act

that a respondent has already performed. State ex rel. Kirk v. Burcham, 1998-Ohio

224, ¶ 7.  The village argues that relators are attempting to use a mandamus action

to expedite the site-plan-review p process rather than to compel the performance of a

duty required by law.

{¶ 35} We are unpersuaded by the village’s argument. The evidence before

us shows that the village began the site-plan-review process under the Lordstown

Codified Ordinances and the Overview utilized by the village in administering

Lordstown Cod.Ord. 1115.04. However, that process halted on January 5, 2026,

when the village council enacted the moratorium on data centers. The moratorium

expressly suspended any obligation by the Planning and Zoning Office or any

Lordstown official to act on an application for a zoning permit or conduct a site

plan review for data centers. Lordstown Ord. No. 1-2026, § 3, available at Village

of Lordstown, https://www.lordstown.com/wp-content/uploads/1-2026.pdf

(accessed Sept. 17, 2026) [https://perma.cc/48BU-9QWX].  And in accordance

with the moratorium, the council also voted not to approve the village engineer’s

proposed work authorization, which is required before the site-plan-review process

can proceed. Relators are therefore seeking a writ of mandamus to compel an act

that has not yet been performed—d—n—namely, reviewing Bristolville’s site plan. See

State ex rel. Martin Marietta Materials, Inc. v. Trenton, 2024-Ohio-6054, ¶ 28-30

(12th Dist.) (granting writ of mandamus ordering a city to act on a site-plan

application under the process set forth in the city’s code).  Therefore, we reject the

village’s argument that it is performing the act sought to be compelled.

B. Relators’ Mandamus Claim

{¶ 36} Relators rely on Lordstown Cod.Ord. 1115.04 as the source of the

purported clear legal right and duty they seek to enforce in mandamus. Because

Lordstown Cod.Ord. 1115.04(c) states that the Planning Commission “shall review

the site plan” under the site-plan-review guidelines, relators argue, the village has

a duty to complete the site-plan review and act on Bristolville’s submission.  For

its part, the village contends that Bristolville does not have a vested legal right to

have the village move forward with the site-plan review and that, in any event,

Bristolville has an adequate remedy in the ordinary course of the law through an

administrative appeal.

{¶ 37} Lordstown’s site-plan-review process has been placed on hold as it

relates to the construction of data centers. Lordstown’s village council enacted a

moratorium on “the granting/issuing of any new permits, as well as the receipt and

processing of applications for permits” under the Lordstown Codified Ordinances.

Lordstown Ord. No. 1-2026, available at Village of Lordstown,

https://www.lordstown.com/wp-content/uploads/1-2026.pdf (accessed Sept. 17,

2026) [https://perma.cc/48BU-9QWX]; Lordstown Ord. No. 29-2026 (extending

the moratorium), available at Village of Lordstown,

https://www.lordstown.com/wp-content/uploads/29-2026-1.pdf (accessed Sept.

17, 2026) [https://perma.cc/NT5M-5JSU]. The moratorium “suspends any

obligations or requirements to act . . . upon an application for a permit and/or in the

site plan review approval process for Data Centers, during the time when th[e]

moratorium shall be in effect.” Lordstown Ord. No. 1-2026, § 3; Lordstown Ord.

No. 29-2026, § 3. As long as a duly enacted moratorium ordinance is in effect, the

village does not have a clear legal duty to proceed with a review of Bristolville’s

site plan, unless the moratorium is unenforceable as to Bristolville’s submission.

See State ex rel. Purdy v. Clermont Cty. Bd. of Elections, 1997-Ohio-278, ¶ 11-15

14

(determining that a clear legal right enforceable in mandamus would exist if a

statute blocking the requested relief were declared unconstitutional).

{¶ 38} Relators contend that the village must move forward with the site

plan-review process for Bristolville’s proposed data center because the moratorium

does not apply to Bristolville’s site-plan submission. Relators argue that they have

“a vested legal right” to have the project reviewed under Lordstown’s zoning

ordinances “as they existed before the Village enacted the moratorium.”

{¶ 39} In invoking their “vested legal right,” relators rely first on Gibson v.

Oberlin, 171 Ohio St. 1 (1960). In Gibson, a building inspector refused to issue a

building permit for construction of a 12-suite apartment building. After the city’s

appellate process upheld the building inspector’s decision, the owner appealed to the

court of common pleas. While the appeal was pending, the city amended its zoning

ordinances to make the owner’s proposed apartment building an impermissible use.

Id. at 2. The common pleas court determined that the law in existence at the time of

the permit application controlled and that the property owner therefore was entitled

to the permit. On appeal, however, the court of appeals reversed, determining that

the amended ordinances applied to the owner’s permit application.

{¶ 40} The property owner appealed to this court, which framed the issue as

“whether the issuance of a building permit is governed by the law in effect at the time

of the application for such permit or is governed by an amendment to such law

enacted during the pendency of litigation.”  Id.  We held that although property

owners have no vested right in the continuance of any particular regulation, “once

regulations have been established, and so long as such regulations are in force, the

state and its subdivisions are as much bound as the people to abide by such

regulations.” Id. at 5. Accordingly, we reversed the court of appeals’ judgment, id.

at 6, holding that “[w]here a property owner complies with all the legislative

requirements for the procurement of a building permit and his proposed structure falls

within the use classification of the area in which he proposes to build it, he has a right

15

to such permit, and there is a duty on the part of the officer charged therewith to issue

it,” id. a at paragraph one of the syllabus. Such an owner’s right to the permit b becomes

“vested . . . upon the filing of the application for the permit.” Id. at 6.  Based on this

principle, relators argue that Bristolville is entitled to have the village move forward

with the site-plan review for B Bristolville’s proposed data center without regard to the

moratorium.

{¶ 41} Relators misapply Gibson. That case stands for the proposition that

the property owner must have complied with all the legislative requirements for

procuring a permit as of the date the owner applied for the permit. Id. at paragraph

two of the syllabus. Bristolville cannot avoid Lordstown’s data-center moratorium

under this rule, however, because it has not yet applied for a permit to begin

construction, much less met all legislative requirements for obtaining such a permit.

Under the Lordstown Codified Ordinances, the Planning Commission’s approval of

a site plan is a necessary, preliminary step to obtaining a zoning permit. Lordstown

Cod.Ord. 1115.02 and 1115.04(c).  Bristolville is essentially asking to apply a vested

rights rule to the site-plan review process. But under Gibson, Bristolville does not

have a vested right to the application of any particular zoning ordinance until it has

met t the legislative requirements to obtain a permit.

{¶ 42} Relators also contend that this court’s holding in State ex rel.

Fairmount Ctr. Co. v. Arnold, 138 Ohio St. 259 (1941), cuts in their favor. In

Fairmount Ctr., a property owner obtained a permit to construct a commercial

building, but the permit expired before construction began. After the permit’s

expiration, the city council introduced an ordinance to rezone the owner’s property

to residential use. Id. at 260. The council simultaneously passed an emergency

ordinance that prohibited the issuance of a building permit during the pendency of

any legislation that would, if adopted, prohibit any use covered by the desired permit.

Id. After the passage of the emergency ordinance, the property owner applied for a

second permit to construct the same building for which the first permit had expired.

16

The city’s building inspector refused to issue the second permit. Id. at 261. The

owner would have been entitled to the second permit but for the emergency

ordinance. Id.

{¶ 43} The owner then filed an action for a writ of mandamus in the court of

appeals, seeking an order compelling the building inspector to issue the second

permit. While that action was pending, the city passed the ordinance to rezone the

owner’s property. The court of appeals denied the writ, and the owner appealed to

this court, challenging both the rezoning ordinance and the “stop-gap” emergency

ordinance that prohibited the issuance of the permit while the zoning-amendment

ordinance was pending. Id. at 261. On appeal, ththis court framed the issue as whether

the city had “the right, by stop-gap ordinance, to hold off [the owner] until it could

put into effect an ordinance rezoning [the owner’s] property.” Id. We held that the

stop-gap ordinance was invalid because it had not been adopted in accordance with

Section 4366-11 of the General Code, which required, among other things, that the

proposed zoning change be submitted to the planning commission for report and to

hold a public hearing on whether the change should be made. Id. at 264-265.

Accordingly, we reversed the judgment of the court of appeals and remanded the case

for further proceedings. Id. at 266.

{¶ 44} Relators argue that Fairmount Ctr. supports their position that

Lordstown’s data-center-moratorium ordinance may not be applied to suspend the

village’s site-plan review of the Bristolville project. They argue that in Fairmount

Ctr., this court applied the prohibition on retroactive laws in Article II, Section 28 of

the Ohio Constitution to the stop-gap ordinance. Likening the stop-gap ordinance in

Fairmount Ctr. with Lordstown’s moratorium ordinance, relators contend that the

moratorium cannot stop the village from moving forward on Bristolville’s site plan.

{¶ 45} As with Gibson, relators misapply Fairmount Ctr.  In that case, the

property owner would have been entitled to a construction permit but for the city’s

passage of the stop-gap ordinance. Id. at 261. In contrast, here, as explained above,

17

Bristolville has not satisfied the requirements for obtaining a permit. Moreover,

contrary to relators’ argument, we did not hold that the stop-gap ordinance in

Fairmount Ctr. was invalid as a retroactive law under Article II, Section 28 of the

Ohio Constitution. “Irrespective of whether this section is a limitation upon a

municipal council,” we held, “the retroactive effect given to [the rezoning ordinance]

by the emergency enactment of [the stop-gap ordinance] deprive[d] [the property

owner] of its property rights without due process of law.” Id. at 266.  In this case,

however, the moratorium is not giving retroactive effect to any zoning change,

because relators—u—unlike the property owner in F Fairmount Ctr.—h—have no entitlement

to a permit even under the premoratorium law.

{¶ 46} Under a close reading of the cases, neither Gibson n nor F Fairmount Ctr.

supports relators’ request for a writ of mandamus ordering the village to move

forward on Bristolville’s site-plan submission. In both of those cases, the relief

sought by the property owner was the issuance of a building permit that had been

denied notwithstanding the property owner’s having satisfied the legislative

requirements for obtaining the permit under the zoning laws in effect at the time of

application. In contrast, here, Bristolville has not met the legislative requirements

for obtaining a permit and therefore has no vested right to enforcement of any

particular zoning ordinance.

{¶ 47} Relators also contend that they should enjoy a vested right to

Lordstown’s premoratorium zoning ordinance as a matter of public policy. They

argue: “To hold that Bristolville’s right to proceed under then-existing law does not

vest until after the Planning Commission approves the site plan . . . would eliminate

all protection that a zoning applicant has against changes in law throughout the

application process, as only projects that comply with any new such laws could be

deemed vested.” Relators further argue that a ruling in the village’s favor may

encourage localities to engage in dilatory tactics similar to what they say the village

has employed here.

18

{¶ 48} The remedy for relators’ policy concern, however, is not a writ of

mandamus. Even if we agreed with Bristolville’s policy argument, “[c]ourts cannot

create the legal duty that is enforceable in mandamus; the creation of a duty

enforceable in mandamus is a legislative and not a judicial function,” State ex rel.

Vermilion Campaign Commt. for Jean A. Anderson v. Lorain Cty. Bd. of Elections,

2025-Ohio-3250, ¶ 33. And in this case, relators have not shown a legislatively

created r right or duty enforceable in mandamus. Under the moratorium, the village is

under no legal duty to proceed with a site-plan review of Bristolville’s project under

Lordstown Cod.Ord. 1115.04 and relators have not shown a clear legal right to have

Bristolville’s site plan reviewed under the premoratorium zoning ordinance.

Accordingly, we deny the writ without reaching the village’s argument that relators

have an adequate a administrative-appeal r remedy in the ordinary course of the law that

precludes r relief in mandamus.

IV. CONCLUSION

{¶ 49} For the foregoing reasons, we deny relators’ motion for leave to file

rebuttal evidence, grant relators’ motion for judicial notice, and deny the writ.

Writ denied.
