 RECOMMENDED ACTION: Staff recommends City Council approve Change Order No. 1 to SEL Engineering Services

 project budget.


BACKGROUND AND ANALYSIS
The City awarded SEL Engineering Services the engineering and construction management contract for replacement of
the deteriorated transformer foundations at Cartanza Substation. SEL is the engineer of record and produced the sealed
design for the project.
Cartanza is the City's largest substation and the sole point of delivery for Dover's power from PJM. As identified in the
approved CIP, failure at this facility would affect service citywide. Maintaining AT-2 in service throughout construction
is a reliability requirement.
During design development it was determined that no spare transformer at the required voltage and MVA rating is
available to the City. To maintain service, transformer AT-2 must remain energized in a temporary position while
foundation work proceeds. This requires temporary transmission design that was not included in the original scope.
Change Order No. 1 adds this temporary transmission design, including coordination with Delmarva Power, material
specifications and procurement support, and coordination with protection and control engineering. The work is a direct
continuation of SEL's sealed design and cannot be separated from it without introducing engineering liability and schedule
risk.
Under this change order, all scope in SEL's contract now applies solely to transformer AT-2. Construction management
effort originally allocated across both transformers is being applied entirely to AT-2, reflecting the expanded scope
associated with the temporary transmission work. Engineering and construction management for transformer AT-1 will be
procured separately at a later date.

The requested change order exceeds the percentage allowed by the City of Dover Purchasing Policy for the City Manager
to authorize.

Sufficient funds remain within the approved project budget. Expenditures to date total $59,575 with $96,054 encumbered,
leaving an available balance of $744,371. Following approval of this change order, the remaining available balance will
be $672,621.

Introduction
Communities across Delaware and the nation are facing growing housing challenges driven
by rising costs, limited housing supply, and changing household needs. Housing costs 1
have increased faster than household incomes, leaving many Delaware households cost-
burdened—spending more than 30 percent of their income on housing. Further, many
households are severely cost-burdened, spending more than half of their income on
housing (see Dover Housing Affordability section on page 3).

Housing demand is also changing as household sizes and demographics evolve. Today,
approximately one-third of American households consist of a single individual. The share of
households with children has declined signiﬁcantly since the 1970s, and by 2030 one in
ﬁve Americans will be over the age 65. Together, these trends are increasing demand for
smaller homes, more attainable housing options, walkable neighborhoods, and housing
that allows residents to age in place. However, land use regulations often prevent
communities from adapting to meet these changing housing needs.

In 2026, the Delaware General Assembly adopted Senate Bill 23, the Housing for Every
Delawarean Act.3 The legislation requires Delaware counties and municipalities with
populations exceeding 2,000 residents to prepare an affordable housing plan as part of
their comprehensive plan. The law requires communities to assess housing needs, identify
barriers to housing production, establish strategies to increase housing supply and
affordability, and implement land use and zoning measures that support a broader range of
housing opportunities. Affordable housing plans will be reviewed by the Delaware State
Housing Authority as part of the state's comprehensive planning process.

This Report provides an assessment of Dover's current housing conditions, affordability
challenges, and housing needs. It evaluates existing barriers to housing production,
examines opportunities to expand housing choices, and identiﬁes potential land use and
zoning strategies that can help the City respond to changing housing needs while
preserving the character and quality of life that deﬁne Dover.


Overview of Housing in Dover
Dover Housing Affordability:
Housing affordability is a serious issue in Dover. The median monthly housing cost in Dover
is $1,337, meaning that a household would need to make at least $4,456 per month to
afford the median housing cost in Dover without being cost-burdened (2024 ACS 5-year
estimates). The median yearly household income in Dover is $60,199. In Dover, 44% of
households are cost burdened (2024 ACS 5-year estimates).

Both homeowners and renters in Dover experience housing cost burden.4 For homes with a
mortgage, the median monthly housing cost was $1,686, while for owned homes without a
mortgage the median monthly housing cost was $534. In practice, this means that 36%
(1,756) of households with a mortgage and 14% (327) of households without a mortgage
are cost burdened (Figure 1). Taken together, 29% of owner-occupied households are cost
burdened in Dover (2024 ACS 5-year estimates).

While the zoning code identiﬁes which housing types are allowed in each zone, the Planned

review from the Planning Commission. If so, the conditional use process will continue as

then the submission of a site development plan or preliminary subdivision plan for review
and approval by the Planning Commission. The minimum tract size for a PND is 20 acres.


Strategies: Barriers and Potential Code Amendments
Based upon the evaluation completed, the project team developed a Barriers Matrix that
summarizes barriers or obstacles to selected reforms (those identiﬁed in Dover’s
application) within the existing code and process, as well as potential amendments that
could remedy the barriers. The proposed amendments in the matrix do not include the
details of how the amendments could be drafted. Evaluating details of how ordinances
should be crafted will take place as the regulatory analysis is discussed with the Council
Committee of the Whole and Planning Commission during August and September.

Although this report and pilot project focus on housing conditions and regulatory
opportunities, affordable housing cannot be addressed in isolation. Long-term success
requires coordinated planning that integrates housing with land use, infrastructure,
transportation, public services, economic development, and demographic trends.
Consistent with the requirements of Senate Bill 23, a more robust plan for affordable
housing will become an element of the City's broader comprehensive planning process.
Accordingly, the ﬁndings of this pilot project should be viewed as an important ﬁrst step
that informs both near-term regulatory reﬁnements and longer-term community planning
efforts.

Conclusion
Overall, Dover’s selected housing strategies have the potential to accelerate affordable
housing in the City. Allowing ADUs would introduce an affordable housing option that also
promotes aging in place, while maintaining local charm. Expanding where Missing Middle
housing types are permitted uses would enable duplexes, townhouses, and other housing
types to be more common in Dover’s communities. These housing types would be
affordable options, yet still maintain Dover’s character—for example, many duplexes look
almost identical to single family homes. Increasing building heights for multifamily housing
and/or mixed-use developments allows buildings in key areas to have more housing units.
Adding housing density in areas already serviced by businesses and transportation can
lead to affordable and vibrant neighborhoods. Allowing higher density mixed-use
developments can contribute to creating more dense, walkable, and affordable
neighborhoods.

While the previously mentioned strategies address housing types and densities, an
inclusionary zoning program would require that a portion of units in new construction
would meet deﬁned affordability requirements. Typically, such requirements would apply
only to medium and large developments. Inclusionary zoning is a creative way to ensure
that affordable housing options grow alongside market-rate housing options.

In addition to those larger strategies identiﬁed, our review of the development process,
requirements, and associated costs identiﬁed other obstacles in the process that we have
recommended addressing. These include reviewing water usage for apartments to
determine if a lower water and wastewater impact fee would be justiﬁed and simplifying
the City’s active recreation requirements speciﬁcally as they relate to small developments.
The project team presented a recommended framework to the Council Committee of the
Whole and the Planning Commission for revising the recreation requirements, which was
favorably received. The team will draft an ordinance to amend this section of code ahead of
other ordinances, as it can operate independently of other recommendations.

Looking forward, the Rossi Team is excited to hear local feedback on these strategies as we
plan our next steps.

SECTION 1

Purpose
The purpose of this Ordinance is to temporarily suspend the acceptance and approval of
applications for new Large Energy Use Facilities while the City develops comprehensive
regulations governing their location, construction, infrastructure requirements, utility impacts, and
public welfare considerations.

SECTION 2

Definitions:

Large Energy Use Facility

Large Energy Use Facility means a facility that does any of the following:

1. Uses or is projected to use a monthly maximum demand of 75 megawatts or greater at a load
factor of 85% or greater.

2. Uses a monthly maximum instantaneous demand of 100 megawatts or greater.

3. Uses or is able to use a monthly maximum demand of 30 megawatts or greater and is primarily
engaged in providing a service described under code 518210 of the 2022 North American Industry
Classification System.

SECTION 3

Moratorium
Effective immediately upon adoption:
The City shall not:
   •   accept;

   •   approve;

What is PBB?
PBB is a financial methodology
that allocates funds based on how
effectively a program or service
aligns with specific, high-priority
goals. This is instead of simply
repeating previous year-to-year
spending.
While line item budgeting remains
essential for financial control, PBB
builds on that foundation to
connect spending to the services
enabled.

the programs and services

approach that enables leaders to
allocate resources based upon
outcomes, service impact, and
community priorities.
