DATE: July 27, 2026 SUBJECT: Discussion of Potential Data Center Regulations


I. INTRODUCTION


On June 1, 2026, the Town Council adopted a 180-day moratorium on the siting, permitting, construction,

or operation of new, enlarged, or expanded data centers with an electrical capacity of 1 megawatt or

greater (Attachment 1). The moratorium was intended to provide the Town with time to consider

regulations addressing the potential impacts of these facilities, including demands on electrical and water

infrastructure, noise, and other environmental impacts.

At tonight’s workshop, staff are seeking to introduce Councilors to this topic and share a preliminary draft

of potential zoning regulations for the Council’s initial consideration (Attachment 2). Working with staff,

f,

Planning Intern Tyler McGrath prepared this draft after researching the approaches used by other

communities to regulate data centers. This initial draft is intended to present one possible regulatory

framework and help Councilors begin considering key policy questions, including whether and to what

extent data centers should be allowed and what facility sizes may be appropriate for Brunswick. Before

reviewing the draft framework, staff will provide introductory information about data centers and their

potential impacts.

II. ABOUT DATA CENTERS


Data centers are an increasingly prominent land use that has expanded rapidly in recent years, particularly

as demand for cloud computing, digital services, and artificial intelligence has increased. Data centers

provide the physical infrastructure needed to house computer servers and related equipment that store,

process, and distribute large volumes of digital information. Depending on their size and cooling

technology, data centers can require substantial amounts of electricity and water. The larger facilities may

consume electricity on a scale comparable to that used by tens of thousands of homes. Regulations may,

however, require measures to reduce or offset these impacts, such as energy-efficiency measures,

renewable-energy strategies, and closed-loop cooling systems that reduce water consumption.

There are no universally accepted classifications for “small,” “medium,” and “large” data centers.

Municipalities typically establish their own thresholds based on local conditions, anticipated infrastructure

impacts, and regulatory objectives. Staff are seeking the Council’s feedback regarding which, if any, of

these facility scales may be appropriate in Brunswick.

III. SUMMARY OF POTENTIAL APPROACH


Brunswick is a relatively small community and already has an existing data center at Brunswick Landing,

operated by FirstLight, which staff estimate has an electrical demand of approximately 750 kW. Based on

these local conditions, staff used 1 MW as the threshold between minor and major data centers. Under the

preliminary framework, facilities with a maximum electrical demand between 100 kW and less than 1 MW

would be classified as minor data centers, while facilities with a maximum electrical demand of 1 MW or

greater would be classified as major data centers. Both classifications would be allowed only in the Growth

Industrial District and would be subject to separate review processes. Facilities with a maximum electrical

demand of less than 100 kW would not be subject to data-center-specific standards.

Minor data centers would be subject to specific performance standards and require a  a C Conditional Use Permit

approval from the Planning Board. These potential standards would address the following areas:

• Water consumption and closed-loop cooling;

• Energy consumption and efficiency;

• Renewable energy;

• Backup generators;

• Electrical, water, and sewer capacity;

Major data centers would require contract zoning, allowing the Council to review each proposal

individually and determine whether it would provide a public benefit, be consistent with the Comprehensive

Plan, and be appropriate for the proposed location. Any required conditions would be established through

the contract zoning agreement based on the specific characteristics and potential impacts of a proposal.

Staff initially considered whether the draft should prohibit larger-scale data centers altogether. While an

outright prohibition may be an available policy option, such an approach would require further in-depth

analysis of the potential impacts of these facilities. This could include a grid-impact analysis prepared by a

qualified energy professional. Developing that supporting analysis would require additional time and Town

resources. Because the contract zoning approach would retain Council review of any proposed major data

center, staff are seeking direction on whether the Council believes this process would provide sufficient

oversight or whether staff should devote additional resources to evaluating whether an outright prohibition

is warranted.

Staff also received written comments from Tucker Favreau, a Brunswick resident and member of the

Town’s Sustainability Committee, who is knowledgeable about this topic. His perspective is included for

the Council’s reference as Attachment 3.

At this evening’s workshop, staff request the Council’s feedback on the proposed regulatory framework,

particularly whether the proposed electrical-demand thresholds appropriately distinguish minor and major

data centers, whether the review requirements for each category provide an appropriate level of oversight,

and whether staff should further evaluate an outright prohibition of major data centers. Staff will continue

to research the topic and incorporate the Council’s initial guidance into an updated draft for subsequent

review.

Town of Brunswick, Maine Data Center Moratorium


WHEREAS, the Town Council of the Town of Brunswick, Maine, after due consideration,

find pursuant to 30-A M.R.S. § 4356 as follows:

1) There has been a recent increase in p proposals to develop large-scale data centers within

the State of Maine;

2) Data centers consume disproportionate amounts of water and electrical energy, often

equivalent to that used by tens of thousands of homes;

3) Data centers are known to strain local energy grids and result in an increase in residential

utility costs;

4) Data centers can create noise pollution, heat islands, and have significant environmental

impactsrequiring large amounts of water to function;

,

5) The development of data centers within the Town, without appropriate ordinance and

regulatory standards, is likely to result in an overburdening of Town resources and

facilities;

6) The Brunswick’s Land Use Ordinance and other available Town Codes and regulations

are inadequate to p prevent the serious public harm that may result from the development

of data centers in the Town or p provide for the effective review and regulation of the

location and operation of data centers in the Town;

7) The Town requires at least 180 days to gather information regarding the potential effect

of data centers on the local energy grid and environment and to develop and implement

ordinances and regulations necessary to address these development pressures and protect

the public health and safety and welfare of the Town’s residents; and

8) The Town Council finds that the foregoing facts necessitate the imposition of a temporary

moratorium on data centers.

NOW THEREFORE, be it ordained by the Town Council of the Town of Brunswick as

follows:

1) This Data Center Moratorium (“Moratorium”) is hereby enacted pursuant to the authority

granted to Town of Brunswick under 30-A M.R.S. § 3001 and 30-A M.R.S. § 4356 and,

in furtherance thereof, the Town Council hereby declares a moratorium on the siting,

permitting, construction and operation of data centers within the Town of Brunswick;

2) This Moratorium shall take effect immediately upon its adoption by the Town Council

and shall remain in effect for one hundred and eighty (180) days from its effective date,

unless extended by order of the Town Council or repealed or modified by ordinance, for

the express purpose of drafting an amendment to the Brunswick Land Use Ordinance and

other Town codes and regulations to establish appropriate standards for the siting,

permitting, construction and operation of data centers within the Town of Brunswick.

Definitions


Sec. 1.7.2. Definitions


Closed-Loop Cooling System: A system that constantly reuses and recycles an initial load

of water within its operation, significantly reducing the draw on external water sources. A

closed loop system shall not use evaporative cooling and may consist of methods

including, but not limited to, air-cooled (dry) cooling, rear-door heat exchanger cooling,

hybrid dry economizer cooling, direct-to-chip cooling, or immersion cooling.

Data Center: A facility, y, or portion thereofhousing computer systems, servers, networking

,

equipment and other components for the principal commercial use of managing, storing,

processing, or distributing digital data, including applicable server rooms. Data centers are

classified according to the electrical-demand thresholds established in Sections 3.4.1.Y

and 3.4.1.Z.

Electrical Demand: The total power consumed by a facility’s servers, storage, cooling,

ventilation, generators, and other networking devices. For the purposes of data centers, on

site generation shall be included in the calculation of electrical demand, except for on-site

solar generation.

Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE): Determined by calculating the total electrical demand

divided by the total power consumed by the components that process, manage, or store

data. This can help indicate how efficiently a data center runs by showing the amount of

energy consumed for data processing in relation to the energy consumed to support that

processing.

Server Room: A dedicated room or enclosed area containing servers and/or other

computer equipment that is located within and supports functions of a principal use and

relies on the principal use’s existing HVAC and electrical supply.

Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE): Determined by calculating the total water consumed in

one hour divided by the total power consumed by the components that process, manage,

or store data in one hour, r, expressed in liters (L) per kilowatt-hour (kWh). This can help

indicate how efficiently a cooling system consumes water.

I have read through the draft ordinance and have some thoughts and additional references

On the thresholds for minor vs major data centers, I think the current 1MW threshold is a bit

too low to separate minor from major. While I am not personally aware of a formal, widely

accepted standard for these thresholds, I have generally seen focuses on 20 MW+ facilities as

"Major" ones. Though I do think that what constitutes a major facility depends upon the local

environment. If there is appetite for more nuance in the ordinance, I would suggest expanding

the categories a bit and perhaps setting an outright ban on any centers exceeding a certain

threshold (e.g. 50+ or 100+ MW). An expanded category list below that top threshold could

include small, medium, and large facilities under the following bands:

Small: Demand < 5 MW

Medium: 5 MW < Demand < 15 / 20 MW

Large: Demand > 20 MW

Again, this mostly depends upon any desired nuance for differing regulations between each

category of facility and could be overkill depending upon the overall intent of the board.

On the second portion regarding PUE, I think the existing requirement is a bit too lenient

though acknowledge that perhaps the intent is to improve it for larger facilities that have

greater impacts. The European Union has already done quite a bit of benchmarking in this area

and has set future goals nearing 1.2 for new facilities this year. Germany has actually enacted

law, not just goals, stipulating a PUE of <= 1.2 starting this year for new facilities. Below is an

excerpt from one resource discussing some known industry values:

"Google's global fleet averaged PUE 1.09 in 2024, with individual sites reaching 1.04. Meta

reported a fleet average of 1.08, with its Prineville facility approaching 1.06. AWS averaged

1.15 across its owned portfolio, with European sites at 1.04. Microsoft reported 1.16 for

FY2024."

does drop towards a PUE average of 1.54. However, the data shows that much better rates are

achievable and that stringent local requirements should be easily achievable in brand new

facilities. I would recommend a much more aggressive PUE requirement of <= 1.4 for any

newly constructed facilities. Though again, this particular value may be more important for
