SUMMARY STATEMENT:

Every year as required by Washington State law, RCW 35.77.010, each city in Washington must develop

and adopt a 6-Year Transportation Improvement Plan (TIP). This program lays out a plan for improving

and maintaining each respective city’s transportation system encompassing streets, trails and mass transit.

Associated with each project is a financial program to support the proposed improvements. The TIP is a

planning tool required by State law to ensure that each city continually has available advanced plans as a

guide in carrying out a coordinated transportation plan.  The law requires that each city holds an annual

public hearing to adopt a 6‐year TIP.  The adopted TIP is then sent out to adjacent local jurisdictions and

utility districts to coordinate projects, as well as to the State for incorporation into the Statewide TIP

(STIP).

There are several benefits derived from the annual update and adoption of a TIP.  These include:

• The TIP is a management tool for city council and city staff.

• It provides valuable information to committees, commissions, citizens, developers and

businesses interested in the development of the city.

• It assists in leveraging available resources through improved timing of projects and improved

coordination of city projects with those of private and other p public entities.

• It helps to protect a city’s current infrastructure and to plan for new infrastructure.

• Many grant sources require that projects be adopted in the city’s TIP prior to grant application.

The City of North Bend’s (“City”) 2027‐2032 T TIP is part of the framework for the City’s Capital Investment

Program (CIP).  The TIP is included in the City’s overarching CIP as the CIP includes parks projects,

facilities projects, water projects, sanitary sewer/WWTP projects, and surface water / storm drainage

projects.  Essentially, the TIP is a plan to improve public transportation facilities.  North Bend’s TIP is a 6‐

year plan that identifies capital projects, their costs, and funding sources.  Staff prepares the TIP and

presents it to City Council each year for review and adoption.

Back in 2013, City staff assigned a project number to each transportation capital project. The ‘T-xxx’

numbers shown below are not project priorities, they are tracking numbers. Project priority numbers

are different, as they change from year-to-year depending upon what projects were completed, what

projects were removed, and changing priorities from City Council and City staff. Priority numbers are

shown on the attached TIP Map and TIP Table. Below is a list of projects that have been completed in

recent years (I), active construction projects (II), and significant changes from last year’s TIP (III).

a 5-leg roundabout (Starfish) at the intersection of NBW and NW 8th Street. One leg of the roundabout is

a new road currently in design called the South Fork Avenue Extension (“Nintendo Bypass”).

constructs a 200-foot long bridge that will provide much safer access for the public over South Fork

Snoqualmie River compared to the existing SR-202 bridge. New bridge will also function as a utility

(sewer pipe) carrier. As such, the bridge is a highly rated transportation capital project, because the

bridge will carry the important Meadowbrook sewer pipe which is being built 2025-2026. Bridge is

located north of vehicular SR-202 bridge. Bridge is located west of the WWTP frontage. Bridge will be

owned, operated and maintained by the City.

SUMMARY STATEMENT:

Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) are large-scale power storage solutions that capture electrical

energy during low-demand periods and discharge based on residential and commercial demand. These

systems typically use lithium-ion technology housed in modular containers. According to the US

Environmental Protection Agency, BESS can help stabilize electrical grids but also pose potential public

health and safety risks due to b battery fires, fire emissions, and long-term environmental impacts.

To better understand the benefits and risks of BESS, the City of North Bend is proposing a t twelve-month

moratorium to determine how BESS would impact the City. The p proposed ordinance directs staff and the

Planning Commission t to initiate the ordinance’s w work plan, develop findings and r recommendations about

the potential p public health and safety i impacts o of BESS, a and to work in c consultation with other governments

and agencies such as Eastside Fire and Rescue, the Puget Sound Clean Air Agency, the King County

Sheriff’s Office, King County, the Washington State Energy Facility Site Evaluation Council, electricity

utility providers, and the Snoqualmie Indian Tribe. Findings and recommendations shall be delivered to

the City Council prior to the end of the 12-month moratorium period. The moratorium may be renewed

for one or more six-month periods as necessary if a subsequent public hearing is held and findings of fact

are made prior to each renewal.

During the effective period of this p proposed m moratorium, the City will not accept, process, and/or approve

applications for land development proposals relating to or incorporating BESS, including but not limited

to, building permits, clearing and grading permits, short plats, subdivisions, binding site plans, site plan

reviews, conditional use permits, reasonable use exceptions, variances from NBMC Title 18 regulations,

or deviations from any City of North Bend engineering design and construction standards or other

technical codes.

WHEREAS, recent events in the Snoqualmie Valley community have informed the City

of North Bend (“City”) and the public of the emerging use of “battery energy storage

systems;” and

WHEREAS, battery energy storage systems refer to systems designed to store electrical

energy for later use, and commonly consist of batteries, power conversion systems, and

control equipment but can vary widely in their uses and scale; and

WHEREAS, battery energy storage systems are commonly used to store energy produced

by renewable energy sources for use during periods of higher demand, to stabilize

fluctuations in the electrical grid, or to serve as backup energy sources; and

WHEREAS, the City’s existing zoning and land use regulations do not contemplate

battery energy storage systems and, pursuant to NBMC Table 18.10.030(5.00), otherwise

permit minor utility and major utility facilities, including electrical distribution substations,

in all zones within the City; and

WHEREAS, the large number of batteries utilized in battery energy storage systems and

the variety of potentially harmful chemicals and compounds involved present potential

risks of thermal runaway, explosion, chemical fires, environmental contamination,

negative impacts on ecological functioning, noise, and aesthetic impacts, all of which are

not well understood by the City and its staff because of the emerging nature of battery

energy storage systems; and

WHEREAS, the City wishes to better understand battery storage systems including, but

not limited to: (i) the potential risks and mitigating measures involved; (ii) the scale and

diversity of battery energy storage systems; and (iii) the concerns and potential impacts on

the community and the City’s tribal and agency partners; and

Re: Notice of Intent to Commence Annexation Proceedings

Dear Council,

On March 30, 2026, the City received from proponent Craig Glazier a Notice of Intention to Commence

Annexation Proceedings (“Notice of Intent to Annex”), attached. To our knowledge, Mr. Glazier has met

the requirement for signatures representing 10% of the assessed valuation of the area to be considered

for annexation, consistent with RCW 35A.14.120.

After a Notice of Intent to Annex has been received  (RCW 35A.14.120), the City Council must hold a

meeting with the initial petitioners within 60 days of the submittal of the Notice of Intent to Annex to

determine the following:

1. Whether the City will accept, reject, or geographically modify the proposed annexation;

2. Whether the City will require the simultaneous adoption of a proposed zoning regulation, if such a

proposal has been prepared and filed; and

3. Whether the City will require the assumption of all or any portion of existing City indebtedness by

the area to be annexed.

This has been scheduled for the May 19, 2026 City Council meeting. Because of the requirements in

RCW 35A.14.120, the Notice of Intent to Annex must go before the full Council for action per the above,

regardless of Council Committee review. The CED Committee reviewed this at their April 21 meeting

and recommended it be brought to the April 28 Council Workstudy meeting for Council discussion on

the above requirements, and to consider whether the Council wishes to geographically modify the

proposed area for annexation.

Of note, the required action at the May 19 Council meeting is not to approve the annexation, only to

consider whether to authorize commencing annexation proceedings, which would then initiate

additional public process for the proponent to collect signatures on an annexation petition to eventually

come to Council for decision on the annexation.

If Council chooses to authorize the annexation proceedings, the next steps would be as follows:

1. The applicant would need to obtain the required signatures on an annexation petition from

property owners representing 60% of the assessed value in the designated annexation area.

2. The King County Auditor would then determine the sufficiency of the annexation petition and

signatures pursuant to RCW 35A.01.040.

3. The City would provide public notification and set a hearing date for the proposed annexation.

4. Staff would prepare analyses of the proposed annexation for Council’s consideration before

Council votes on an annexation ordinance in a subsequent meeting.

ANALYSIS

Description of Proposed Annexation Area:

The proposed annexation area (PAA) includes 46 properties along SE 123rd Street, SE 125th Street, 412th

AVE SE, 413rd Ave SE, and 415th Ave SE. A complete listing of the 46 properties, by address, is included in

the appendix to this report. The PAA is located in unincorporated King County and immediately adjacent

to city limits.

The PAA currently receives the following services:

- Police protection services from the King County Sheriff’s Office.

- Fire protection services from Fire District #38, under contract through Eastside Fire and Rescue

- Roads maintenance services from the King County Roads Division.

- Water services from the City of North Bend. As the PAA is not within City boundaries, water

service customers in the PAA are billed outside City water customer. Outside city utility rates are

65% higher than inside city rates.

- Presently, the PAA does not receive any sewer utility services. Sewer services are provided

through individual property septic systems (except for one property which is served by City of

North Bend sewer utility, as a result of a previous failure of their septic system, which the

property connected to City sewer as required by King County).

- Stormwater services from King County, paid through the County’s property tax assessment.

- Property tax levy of $10.22 per thousand of assessed value.

- Flood insurance premiums are currently based on King County CRS class 2, with an estimated

annual discount of 40%. The CRS (Community Rating System) is administered by the federal

FEMA agency and applies to buildings that are compliant with the minimum floodplain

management regulations within the community.

If the PAA is approved and annexed into the City, the following services would change:

- Police protection services would be provided by the City of North Bend under its contract

through the King County Sheriff’s Office. This service is funded by general City taxes.

- Fire protection services would be provided by the City of North Bend under its contract through

Eastside Fire and Rescue. This service is funded by general City taxes.

- Roads maintenance services would be provided by the City of North Bend Public Works

Department. This service is funded by general and transportation benefit district City taxes.

- Water services would continue to be provided by the City of North Bend. However, the account

status would be revised and billed as an inside City water customer.

- While the City of North Bend provides sewer utility services, the PAA would continue to receive

services through individual property septic systems. To the extent individual septic systems fail

in the future, those failed properties would be required to connect to City sewer services should

it become available in the PAA.

- Stormwater services from the City of North Bend, billed as an inside City water customer.

- Property tax levy of $9.03 per thousand of assessed value. The property tax levy would fall due

to the elimination of the County Road Tax ($1.25) and the elimination of the Fire District 38

- Utility tax levy of 6.0% on water, sewer, electricity, natural gas, and telephone services and 5.0%

on cable television services.

- Flood insurance premiums would be revised and based on North Bend CRS class 5, which has a

smaller discount of 25%.

- No change in sales taxes is expected as residents are expected to continue to shop locally.

Analytic approach:

The fiscal impact analysis included evaluating the immediate financial and level of service impacts of the

changes as outlined above, from a resident’s perspective and from the City’s perspective. This includes:

- Impact upon property tax payments and property tax revenues.

- Impact upon water, sewer, and stormwater service rate payments and revenues.

- Impact upon utility taxes payments and revenues.

- Impact to the City of North Bend Public Works operations and staff.

- Impact to the King County Sheriff’s Office and their provision of services and charges assessed to

the City of North Bend.

- Impact to Eastside Fire and Rescue and their provision of services and charges assessed to the

City of North Bend.

Fiscal Impact from a Residents Perspective – net savings of $713/household:

If the PAA is approved and annexed into the City, it is estimated that the typical resident would

experience a net cost savings as a result of a decline in property taxes, as the property tax levy rate

would decline by $1.19 per thousand assessed value, and a decline in water utility billings as residents

would be billed at inside city rates, rather than outside rates. These decreases would be partially offset

by an increase in collective utility taxes paid on public and private utility services. Individual flood

insurance premiums would increase as the CRS discount would be reduced from 40% to 25%.

Residents would receive road maintenance and stormwater maintenance services from the City of North

Bend. Residents would receive police services from the King County Sheriff’s Office through the City of

North Bend’s contract with the KCSO. Residents would receive fire services from Eastside Fire and

Rescue through the City of North Bend’s contract with EFR.

Fiscal Impact from the City of North Bend’s Perspective – net revenue of $15,000 annually:

If the PAA is approved and annexed into the City, it is estimated that the City of North Bend would

realize a net additional annual revenue as a result of additional property taxes and utility taxes. These

additional revenues would be partially offset by lowered water utility service rate revenues and a minor

increase in City contractual payments to Eastside Fire and Rescue. About 85% of EFR’s charges are based

on the total assessed valuation of the City. To place this change in in context, the City’s 2026 payment to

EFR is about $2.0 million.

No changes to the amount of sales taxes paid is expected, as residents would continue to shop locally.

No changes to sewer utility billings as residents would continue to be served by individually owned

septic systems. Residents would become a part of the City’s stormwater utility rate base and pay

stormwater utility rates, the estimate of which is included in the above figure. The City would not see a

fiscal impact from the change in the flood insurance CRS discount as this discount would apply to private

party flood insurance policies.

SUMMARY STATEMENT:

Amendments are proposed to Chapter 20.05 of the N North Bend Municipal Code (“NBMC”),entitled d “Staff

Report, Public Hearing and Notice of Decision.” The proposed amendments incorporate recently adopted

State legislation in RCW 36.70B.080 concerning permitting timeframes, as amended through Senate Bill

5290, laws of 2023. The effect of the proposed amendments is to shorten the timeframe for project review

for most permits, with the following below noted exceptions.

As provided under RCW 36.70B.080(1)(a)(3), the timeframe in NBMC Chapter 20.05 to issue decisions

for projects involving a public hearing has been expanded from 120 days to 170 days. In addition,

consistent with the allowance to extend the permitting timeframe under RCW 36.70B.080(1)(e), the City

is proposing to extend the review timeframe for certain types of project permits from the State’s 100-day

default deadline provided in RCW 36.70B.080(1)(ii) to 170 days. These include projects that require an

Environmental Impact Statement (“EIS”), shoreline permits, and critical areas review. Project permitting

involving EIS, shoreline permit, and critical areas review tend to be complex and are likely to receive

comments from the public, state agencies, and tribes. Because of this, they often take more time to process

than other types of project permits. Shoreline permits also require additional public notification

timeframes, as required by the state.

Several additional “housekeeping” edits are included within the amendments to NBMC 20.05 to provide

clarity to provisions in Chapter 20.05 regarding ex-parte communication, aligning the decision timelines

with those in the City’s Hearing Examiner Rules of Procedure, and d the method to calculate time periods

for issuance of decisions.

The Planning Commission held a Public Hearing on March 18, 2026, and no written or verbal comments

were received on the proposed amendments. On that date, the Planning Commission recommended

approval of the proposed amendments to the City Council, as further detailed in the attached Planning

Commission report and recommendation.

Staff recommend approval of the proposed amendments to Chapter 2 20.05 N NBMC to remain consistent with

State law.

Regional Fire Authority: An RFA, authorized under RCW 52.26, is an independent municipal

corporation with its own taxing authority. It is formed when two or more governmental entities

that each individually provide fire protection services, are located within reasonable proximity,

and merge to provide fire protection services. Funding for an RFA includes a property tax levy

The following responses are intended to clarify how fire and emergency medical services are

1. Several Councilmembers would like better visuals, including a map showing FD10, FD38, EFR/North

Bend boundaries, service areas, and station locations. Who serves where, and do the agencies

routinely back each other up?

Fire service boundaries and operational response areas are related, but they are not the same.

• Fire service boundaries identify which agency has legal and financial responsibility for service.

• Operational response areas are based on sending the closest appropriate resource, such as

an engine, aid car, ladder, tender, or brush truck, depending on the nature of the emergency.

Our regional dispatch and response system is designed to provide the fastest appropriate response

to a 911 caller. As a result, crews cross jurisdictional boundaries daily. Eastside Fire and Rescue

(EFR) resources respond into Snoqualmie, Fall City, Snoqualmie Pass, unincorporated King

County, and areas outside formal fire district boundaries, including Washington State Department

of Natural Resources (DNR) land, when dispatched as the closest appropriate resource.

Neighboring agencies also respond into EFR service areas when they are closest or otherwise

appropriate for the incident.

For example, over the past three years, Snoqualmie Fire responded into the EFR service area 1,324

times, while EFR responded into Snoqualmie’s area 486 times. Station 87 also responds to

Snoqualmie Pass as part of a mutual aid agreement.

Station 87 is a high-activity regional resource. For example, in 2025, Station 87 responded to

approximately 1,962 calls, or on average about 5.37 calls per day, including calls inside the City, in

Fire District 38, and into neighboring jurisdictions through the regional response system. On

average, EFR response times in 2025 was 9:24 minutes per call in suburban areas.

determine which assets are owned by Fire District 38, North Bend, or EFR, and how those assets

should be transferred, assigned, leased, or otherwise addressed.

7. What are the cost implications for city residents? Would the City reduce its tax rate, delay future

increases, or is there another cumulative resident impact?

This is one of the most important questions for elected officials and residents. The resident impact

depends on two separate decisions:

• First, what funding mechanism the RFA adopts, including whether a fire benefit charge is

utilized.

• Second, whether the City will redirect its current tax capacity that was used to fund fire services

to other City services.

If fire services move to an RFA, the City’s direct fire-service expenditure would be removed.

However, whether that results in a direct City tax-rate reduction, a delay in future City tax

increases, or whether the existing tax capacity is preserved and redirected to other City services is

a City Council policy decision.

For that reason, the most helpful analysis is an “all-in taxpayer impact” chart showing the current

model compared with the proposed RFA model. The chart should identify the City budget impact,

the RFA funding impact, and the estimated net impact to a typical resident.
