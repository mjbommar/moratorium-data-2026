DATA CENTERS

           What is a data center?

Data centers are specialized infrastructure facilities that house computer servers and related equipment used
to store, process, manage, and transmit digital information.

They support many systems we use every day, including:
• Cloud storage and computing
• Websites and mobile applications
• Artificial intelligence
• Banking and financial transactions
• Healthcare systems
• Communications
• Manufacturing and logistics
• Transportation and public safety systems

However, data centers are not ordinary offices or warehouses.

They combine high energy and infrastructure demands with continuous cooling, backup power generation,
mechanical noise, relatively low employment density, etc.                                        Page 5 of 191


                Current Conditions

Ypsilanti’s current zoning ordinance does not define data centers, meaning they may fall under an already
established land use category in the ordinance (essential services or unclassified uses)

Establishing regulations gives the city a stronger hand and legal position rather than silence. The ordinance as
proposed would allow the city to:
• Decide where data centers are permitted;
• Evaluate land use impacts and neighborhood compatibility;
• Establish measurable operational standards; and
• Advance guiding values within the Master Plan and coordinate reinvestment in vacant spaces.

SUMMARY

The City of Ypsilanti is committed to improving its zoning systems, and upholding its status as a
Redevelopment Ready Community, by establishing a clearer, more efficient process for reviewing
revisions to approved Planned Unit Developments (PUDs). The amendment introduces the following:
    1. Clearly defining public benefits
    2. Establish concise guidelines for development agreements
    3. Require a mandatory preapplication meeting with City staff and a preliminary review by the
        Planning Commission to provide guidance to the applicant and enhance time-efficiency.
    4. Clearly defining revisions by introducing a tiered review structure that distinguishes between
        major revisions, minor revisions requiring Planning Commission review, and minor
        administrative revisions that may be approved by the City Planner.
    5. Lastly, establishing a separate review process for Adaptive Reuse Planned Unit Developments to
        ease regulatory burdens on applicants looking to revitalize an existing structure/space.


BACKGROUND

Following the installation of Solar EV Arc charging stations at 218 N. Adams, an adaptive reuse planned
unit development, staff were concerned about the regulatory burden of requiring a site plan review from
the Planning Commission, when a site, that is not a planned unit development, would’ve only required a
sketch plan and administrative review. The city has largely been an advocate for alternative energy
sources and encouraging the adaptive reuse of historic sites. These amendments are believed to alleviate
the ordinance’s current regulatory burden reflect the community’s values.

Article VII: Supplementary Regulations

Division 1: Planned Unit Development

Section 122-700. Description and Purpose

   (a) It is the purpose of this article to provide guidelines for development or redevelopment which
       is planned as a unit. Toward this end, it is the intent of these regulations to allow flexibility in
       the regulation of land development; encourage innovation in land use and variety in design,
       layout, and type of structures constructed; achieve economy and efficiency in the use of land,
       energy, public services, and utilities; encourage useful open space; provide better housing,
       employment, shopping opportunities, compatibility of design, and use between neighboring
       properties; facilitate the preservation and reuse of historic structures; and encourage
       development that is consistent with the City's master land use plan.
   (b) The approval of a planned unit development application requires an amendment to this chapter
       to revise the Zoning Map and designate the subject property as "PUD, planned unit
       development." An approval granted under this article, including all aspects of the final site
       development plan and conditions imposed on it, constitutes an inseparable part of this chapter.
   (c) The provisions of this article are not intended as a device for ignoring this chapter, the specific
       standards set forth in this chapter, or the planning upon which it has been based. Provisions of
       this article are intended to result in land development substantially consistent with the zoning
       standards generally applied to the proposed uses, allowing for modifications and departures
       from generally applicable standards in accordance with the guidelines of this article to insure
       appropriate, fair, and consistent decision-making.

Section 122-701. Permitted Uses

In a planned unit development, the principal uses which are permitted are based on the underlying
zoning district. City Council may also consider, upon Planning Commission recommendation, the City
of Ypsilanti Master Plan as a basis for principal uses permitted in a planned unit development.

Section 122-702. Requirements for Planned Unit Developments

The following requirements must apply to all planned unit developments:

   (a) Unified control. The proposed development must be under single ownership or control such
       that there is a single person or entity having responsibility for completing the project in
       conformity with this chapter. The applicant must provide legal documentation of a single
       ownership or control in the form of agreements, contracts, covenants, and deed restrictions
       which indicate that the development can be completed as shown on the plans, and further, that
       all portions of the development that are not to be maintained or operated at public expense will
       continue to be operated and maintained by the developers or their successors.
   (b) Minimum size. The minimum size of a planned unit development in the Walkable Urban
       Districts is one half-acre of contiguous land. The minimum size of a planned unit development
       in the Use Based Districts is one acre of contiguous land.
   (c) Applicable base regulations. Unless waived or modified in accordance with the procedures and
       standards set forth in this article, the yard, bulk, parking, loading, landscaping, lighting, general
       provisions, and all other standards set forth in this chapter for the uses listed below must be
       applicable for uses proposed as part of a planned unit development:
            (1) Multiple-family residential uses must comply with the regulations applicable in the
                MD multiple dwelling residential district.
            (2) Commercial and office uses must comply with the regulations applicable in the C -
                Center district.
            (3) Manufacturing uses must comply with the regulations applicable in the PMD -
                Production, Manufacturing, and Distribution district.
            (4) Mixed uses must comply with the regulations applicable for each individual use, as
                outlined above, except that if conflicts exist between provisions, the regulations
                applicable to the most dominant use must apply.
            (5) To encourage flexibility and creativity in development consistent with the planned
                development concept, departures from compliance with the base regulations may be
                granted by the City Council, upon recommendation of the Planning Commission, as a
                part of the approval of the planned development. For example, such departures may
                include modifications of lot dimensional standards, setback requirements, density
                standards, parking and landscaping requirements, and similar requirements. Such
                departures may be approved only on the condition that they will result in a higher
                quality of development than would be possible using conventional zoning standards.
   (d) Street access. Each lot, main building, and principal use within a planned development district
       must have vehicular access to a public street. Adequate provision must be made for dedications
       of land for streets and essential services.
   (e) Usable open space. The proposed development must contain at least as much open space as
       would otherwise be required by the existing underlying zoning.
   (f) Landscaping and maintenance of common areas. All required yards and common areas must be
       landscaped and adequately and permanently maintained by the property owner, tenant, or
       organization responsible for maintaining common areas. Through an irrevocable conveyance,
       such as deed restrictions or covenants that run with the land, the developer must assure that all



                                                                                                         Page 17 of 191


        yards and common areas will be developed in accordance to the site plan and not changed to
        another use.
    (g) Additional considerations. During review of a proposed planned development, the Planning
        Commission must take into account the following considerations which may be relevant to a
        particular project: perimeter setbacks and screening; thoroughfares, drainage, as provided for in
        best management practices as appropriate, and utility design; underground installation of
        utilities; insulating pedestrian circulation from vehicular thoroughfares and ways; achievement
        of an integrated development with respect to signage, lighting, landscaping and building
        materials; and noise reduction mechanisms, particularly in cases where nonresidential uses
        adjoin off-site residentially-zoned property.
Section 122-703. Planned Adaptive Reuse Projects

A planned unit development that involves adaptive reuse of an existing structure that is located in a
residential zoning district, but that was originally constructed for non-residential use, may be reviewed
as a planned adaptive reuse project at the request of the applicant. The following requirements must
apply to a planned adaptive reuse project in addition to the requirements applied to all planned unit
developments:

    (a) Eligibility. The project must involve the reuse of an existing structure that is located in a
        residential zoning district, that was constructed for non-residential use.

    (b) Minimum size. Notwithstanding §122-702(b), no minimum size is required for a planned
        adaptive reuse project.

    (c) Permitted uses. Notwithstanding §122-701, a planned adaptive reuse project may include any
        uses determined by the City Council, upon Planning Commission recommendation, to be
        appropriate for the site and compatible with adjacent uses and zoning districts, with the master
        plan, and with any subarea plans.

Section 122-704. Application Requirements

An application for a planned unit development must contain the following:

    (a) Cover letter signed by the applicant and owner holding an equitable interest in the property.
    (b) Legal description showing the location and acreage of the property.
    (c) General description of proposed development, including a timetable of development and a list
        of departures from the regulations of this chapter which will be required.
    (d) Site plan at a scale of one inch equals 50 feet or larger, prepared in accordance with §122-309.
        Additional information on the site plan must include:
            1) A schedule of total land areas devoted to each type of use, useable floor areas, density
                calculations, number and types of units, and building ground coverage.
            2) Open space areas, indicating the proposed uses and improvements for such areas.
            3) Architectural sketches showing building heights, external wall finishes, location of
                building entry ways, lighting elements, and other architectural features.
            4) Copy of agreements, covenants, or deed restrictions which will assure that the
                development will be completed and maintained as shown on the plans by the developer
                and successive owners.
            5) Other information deemed pertinent to the proposed development by the Planning
                Commission or City Council.

Section 122-706. Standards for Approval

In considering any application for a planned unit development, the Planning Commission and City
Council must make their determinations based on the standards for site plan approval in §122-311 and
the following standards:

    (a) Conformance with the planned development concept. The overall design and all uses proposed
        in connection with a planned unit development must be consistent with and promote the intent
        of this article, as well as with specific project design standards set forth in this chapter.
    (b) Recognizable benefits. The planned development will result in recognizable and substantial
        benefits to the ultimate users of the project and to the community in general where such
        benefits would otherwise be unfeasible or unlikely to be achieved.
    (c) Compatibility with adjacent uses. The proposed planned unit development must be designed
        with due regard to its relationship with development on surrounding properties and uses
        thereon, including building heights, setbacks, density, parking, circulation, landscaping, views,
        and other layout features. In particular, consideration must be given to the following:
             (1) The bulk, placement, architecture, and types of materials used in construction of
                 proposed structures.
             (2) The location and screening of vehicular circulation and parking areas in relation to
                 surrounding development.
             (3) The location and screening of outdoor storage, outdoor activity or work areas, and
                 mechanical equipment in relation to surrounding development.
             (4) The hours of operation of the proposed uses.
             (5) Landscaping, preservation of historic features, and other site amenities.
    (d) Impact of traffic. The planned development must be designed to minimize any adverse impact
        of traffic generated by the proposed development. Consideration must be given to the
        following:
             (1) Estimated traffic to be generated by the proposed development.
             (2) Access to major thoroughfares.
             (3) Proximity and relation to intersections.
             (4) Adequacy of driver sight distances.
             (5) Location of and access to off-street parking.
             (6) Required vehicular turning movements.
             (7) Provisions for pedestrian traffic.
             (8) Access to loading and unloading areas.
    (e) Public services. The proposed type and density of use must not result in a material increase in
        the need for public services, facilities, and utilities; including but not necessarily limited to
        water and sewer services, public roads, fire and police protection, and schools. The proposal

Section 122-702. Requirements for Planned Unit Developments

The following requirements must apply to all planned unit developments:

   (h) Unified control. The proposed development must be under single ownership or control such

       Districts is one half-acre of contiguous land. The minimum size of a planned unit development

Section 122-711. Adaptive Reuse Planned Unit Development

    (a) Purpose. The Adaptive Reuse Planned Unit Development (ARPUD) is intended to facilitate the
        reuse of existing nonresidential buildings located within residential zoning districts where
        adaptive reuse supports neighborhood compatibility, historic preservation, economic
        revitalization, and the Ypsilanti Master Plan.
    (b) Applicability. ARPUD applies only to existing nonresidential buildings located in residential
        zoning districts.
    (c) Approval Process. Notwithstanding this article, an ARPUD shall be reviewed and approved as
        a Special Land Use under Article III, Division 2, Subdivision II, rather than as a rezoning.
    (d) Flexibility. As part of Special Land Use approval, the Planning Commission may approve
        modifications to dimensional, parking, landscaping, and design standards where necessary to
        accommodate adaptive reuse.
    (e) Approval Standards. In addition to Special Land Use standards, the Planning Commission shall
        find:
            (1) The reuse preserves an existing building of historic, architectural, or community
                 significance, or avoids demolition of a viable structure;
            (2) The proposed use is compatible with surrounding residential uses;
            (3) Site impacts are appropriately mitigated;
            (4) The project advances Master Plan goals.
    (f) Permitted Uses. The Planning Commission may approve any use determined compatible with
        the building, site, and surrounding neighborhood, notwithstanding the underlying district,

Section 122-702. Requirements for Planned Unit Developments

The following requirements must apply to all planned unit developments:

   (o) Unified control. The proposed development must be under single ownership or control such

             (1) Multiple-family residential uses must comply with the regulations applicable in the
                  MD multiple dwelling residential district.
             (2) Commercial and office uses must comply with the regulations applicable in the C -
                  Center district.
             (3) Manufacturing uses must comply with the regulations applicable in the PMD -
                  Production, Manufacturing, and Distribution district.

Section 122-707. Standards for Approval

In considering any application for a planned unit development, the Planning Commission and City
Council must make their determinations based on the standards for site plan approval in §122-311 and
the following standards:

    (s) Conformance with the planned development concept. The overall design and all uses proposed
        in connection with a planned unit development must be consistent with and promote the intent
        of this article, as well as with specific project design standards set forth in this chapter.
    (t) Recognizable benefits. The planned development will result in recognizable and substantial
        benefits to the ultimate users of the project and to the community in general where such
        benefits would otherwise be unfeasible or unlikely to be achieved.
    (u) Compatibility with adjacent uses. The proposed planned unit development must be designed
        with due regard to its relationship with development on surrounding properties and uses
        thereon, including building heights, setbacks, density, parking, circulation, landscaping, views,
        and other layout features. In particular, consideration must be given to the following:
             (1) The bulk, placement, architecture, and types of materials used in construction of
                 proposed structures.
             (2) The location and screening of vehicular circulation and parking areas in relation to
                 surrounding development.
             (3) The location and screening of outdoor storage, outdoor activity or work areas, and
                 mechanical equipment in relation to surrounding development.
             (4) The hours of operation of the proposed uses.
             (5) Landscaping, preservation of historic features, and other site amenities.
    (v) Impact of traffic. The planned development must be designed to minimize any adverse impact
        of traffic generated by the proposed development. Consideration must be given to the
        following:
             (1) Estimated traffic to be generated by the proposed development.
             (2) Access to major thoroughfares.
             (3) Proximity and relation to intersections.
             (4) Adequacy of driver sight distances.
             (5) Location of and access to off-street parking.


                                                                                                        Page 42 of 191


             (6) Required vehicular turning movements.
             (7) Provisions for pedestrian traffic.
             (8) Access to loading and unloading areas.
    (w) Public services. The proposed type and density of use must not result in a material increase in
         the need for public services, facilities, and utilities; including but not necessarily limited to
         water and sewer services, public roads, fire and police protection, and schools. The proposal
         must not place an undue burden upon the subject or surrounding land or property owners and
         occupants or the natural environment.
    (x) Compatibility with master plan and this chapter. The proposed development must be
         compatible with the adopted City master plan and with the spirit and intent of this chapter.
    (y) Economic impact. The proposed development must not result in an unreasonable negative
         economic impact upon surrounding properties.
    (z) Compliance with applicable regulations. The proposed development must be in compliance
         with all applicable federal, state, county, and local laws and regulations.
    (aa) Phasing. Where a project is proposed for construction in phases, the project must be so
         designed that each phase, when completed, must be capable of standing on its own in terms of
         the presence of services, facilities, and open space; and must contain the necessary components
         to ensure protection of natural resources and the health, safety, and welfare of the users of the
         planned unit development and the occupants of the surrounding area.

           (5) The reuse preserves an existing building of historic, architectural, or community

SUMMARY

The Historic Corridor is intended to preserve Ypsilanti's historic development pattern while
accommodating context-sensitive reinvestment and housing opportunities. Because building type
eligibility is tied to minimum lot size requirements, some lots cannot be developed or redeveloped despite
the Master Plan recognizing that smaller homes are an established component of the corridor, creating a
disconnect between the City's adopted planning policies and its zoning regulations.

BACKGROUND

Section 122-465(a) establishes the permitted building types for each form-based zoning district. In the
Historic Corridor, the following building types are permitted:
    • MA: Mansion (Limited)
    • ES: Estate
    • HS: House
    • AH: Apartment House
    • CA: Courtyard Apartments
    • AB: Apartment Buildings (Limited)


                                                                                                          Page 63 of 191


    •   CS: Commercial/Mixed-Use Small
    •   CM: Commercial/Mixed-Use Medium
    •   SC: Single-Store Commercial (Limited)
    •   IT: Institutional (Limited)

The Master Plan identifies the Historic Corridors as, “Historic Corridors connect the centers of the City
with each other and the surrounding neighborhoods. They are dominated by large, historic homes now
used in a variety of ways – residences, office, retail. Houses of worship and other civic buildings also line
these corridors, interspersed with smaller homes. The transportation plan sees restoration of two-way
traffic to the one-way historic corridors of Huron, Hamilton and Cross. It also proposes the extension of
River Street through the Water Street redevelopment area to Factory in the next twenty years. Uses will
remain flexible allowing the historic buildings to accommodate changing markets and traffic
patterns.” It’s clear that the Historic Corridor intends to preserve the City's historic development pattern
but also aims to accommodate reinvestment and evolving modern land uses.

In addition to identifying the Historic Corridors, the Master Plan adopts a City Framework Map that is
intended to preserve and reinforce Ypsilanti's historic development pattern. The Framework Map
recognizes the City's traditional street network, historic lot configurations, and established building forms
as defining characteristics of Ypsilanti's built environment. Collectively, the Historic Corridor policies
and the City Framework Map establish a clear policy direction that future development should respect and
reinforce the City's existing urban fabric while allowing for context-sensitive reinvestment and
incremental growth.

The City's current zoning regulations for the Historic Corridor, however, create a disconnect between
these adopted planning policies and the standards governing new development. Critically, Section 122-
465(b)(2) of the Zoning Ordinance establishes that building type eligibility is determined by minimum lot
size, stating “Lot Size determines Building Type. The size of the lot determines what type of building
may be placed on a lot. For instance, a cottage or a house building type may be constructed on a 5,000
square foot lot, since all of those building types fit within the allowed lot size for those building types, but
an estate building type is not allowed. Maximum lot depth and width do not determine building types,
but all building types must meet the minimum lot width, depth and size.” Admittedly, Staff
recognizes the contradiction established in this section; however, this standard and its contradiction has
enhanced day-to-day zoning administration by effectively reducing regulatory barriers that encourages
reinvestment into our community. Though it is stated that the maximum lot depth and width do not
determine the building type, rather the minimum lot depth and width does, it presents challenges within
the Historic Corridor, as nealy all parcels within this district were created and developed prior to the
adoption of the City's form-based zoning code. As a result, many of the corridor's existing structures
occupy lots that do not satisfy today's minimum lot size requirements for any permitted building type in
this district, and therefore exist as legal nonconformities, despite complying with the minimum lot size
requirements for the cottage or townhouse building types.

Ironically, although smaller homes/lots that would be identified as the cottage building type are
specifically identified in the Master Plan as an established feature of the Historic Corridor, the current
ordinance prohibits construction of comparable building types on similarly sized lots. Consequently, the
development pattern the City seeks to preserve can remain only through existing nonconformities, while
new development is required to conform to standards that differ from the historic development pattern of
these corridors.



                                                                                                            Page 64 of 191


Furthermore, the Historic Corridor is largely built out, with few remaining vacant lots. If the City aims to
encourage continual investment, infill, and densification, it must do so by expanding the possibilities of
what can be developed.

These conflicts have recently become evident through recent development inquiries. The new owner of 12
N. River Street intends to construct a duplex on the vacant lot; however, staff was initially under the
impression that the lot depth was 97 ft, meaning the parcel would not satisfy the minimum lot
requirements for any permitted building type, effectively rendering the property undevelopable without
obtaining a variance. Fortunately, staff was able to confirm that the lot depth was actually 100 ft, meaning
the property barely met the minimum 100 ft lot depth requirement for the house building type.

Unlike 12 N. River, the owner of 105 Babbitt Street, Kyle Kipp, seeks to divide the property and
construct a duplex fronting N. River Street and selling his existing home to a young family; however,
splitting the lot would render the site undevelopable. It is within the City’s best interest to permit the
cottage building type in this zoning district as it would gently increase housing supply through
incremental infill while maintaining a for-sale residential unit that is desperately needed in this City. If the
current owner is able to split and develop part of the lot, the area outlined in red below would be the new
site of a duplex.

These examples suggest that the current building type regulations conflict with the City's adopted
planning policies. The Master Plan expressly recognizes that Historic Corridors are characterized by a

historically platted lots would more closely implement these policies by reinforcing the established urban
fabric, expanding housing opportunities, encouraging reinvestment, and preserving the character that
defines the Historic Corridor.

GENERAL INFORMATION & BACKGROUND

Earlier this year, the Department of Public Works Director, Bonnie Wessler; and Mike Davis Jr, Ypsilanti
Planning Commissioner, PC Liaison to the Non-Motorized Advisory Committee (NMAC), and a planner
for MDOT; approached the Non-Motorized Advisory Committee about tweaking the Non-Motorized
Transportation Plan.

This amendment would establish a new objective encouraging future bridge reconstruction and
replacement projects to incorporate illuminated sidewalks and/or shared-use paths at least 10 feet in
width, to the greatest extent practicable. It specifically identifies the Prospect, Leforge, Cross, Factory,
Forest, and Michigan Avenue bridges as opportunities to explore partnerships and improve connections
within the City’s accessible non-motorized network and to neighboring communities.

The intention of adding this objective to the adopted plan is to strengthen the City’s ability to pursue
outside funding for these improvements by formally identifying shared-use paths and illumination as
priorities when bridge replacement or reconstruction projects arise. Furthermore, the amendment supports
the plan’s broader goals of closing infrastructure gaps, strengthening critical non-motorized connections,
and creating a complete transportation network.

SHIFT TO NON-MOTORIZED MODES (Goal 3)
Shifting transportation users towards non-motorized modes (walking and bicycling) and away from single-occupant vehicles
(automobiles) is commonly referred to as mode-shift.

Mode-shift is a change between one mode (type) of transportation to another. In the context of this plan, mode-shift is a
change from a motor vehicle mode to transit, walking, or bicycling mode. This plan asserts that the City of Ypsilanti will pursue
and align transportation investments that provide complete networks for each mode based upon a complete streets
framework. Furthermore, these networks will provide safe and convenient connections between modes and provide
connections between the city, county, and state systems (City of Boulder, n.d.).

Why?
Creating a transportation network that allows people to shift between modes will benefit all Ypsilanti residents in a multitude
of ways. Some examples include reductions in air pollution and carbon emissions, reduced traffic congestion, increased foot-
traffic for local businesses, and increased positive health outcomes for local residents (C40 Knowledge Hub, n.d.).

   (2) A portion of the building or structure is damaged by fire, wind, flood, or other cause so that

   (3) A part or appurtenance of the building or structure is likely to fall, become detached or

   (5) The building or structure, or a part of the building or structure, because of dilapidation,

   collapse, or some portion of the foundation or underpinning of the building or structure is likely
   to fall or give way.

Sec. 18-167. - Inspection and Lead Clearance Risk Assessment for Lead-Based Paint
Hazards; When Required.
The purpose and intent of this Section is to protect the health and welfare of children who occupy
rental units that contain lead-based paint hazards since exposure to lead can cause serious problems
for children, including learning problems, behavioral problems, developmental delays, and speech
and language problems.

SUBDIVISION A. – General Provisions.
(a) Where interim controls were used to reduce lead-based paint hazards related to a building or
structure required to be registered pursuant to Section 18-131(a), or where a lead inspection reveals
the presence of lead paint on such structure, the owner shall have an annual lead inspection/risk
assessment performed on the structure, and obtain an annual lead-clearance report in accordance
with this Section.
(b) If abatement was used to remove all identified lead hazards, as prescribed herein, the owner
shall have a lead inspection/risk assessment performed on the structure every three (3) years, and
the lead-clearance report shall be valid for three (3) years.
(c) Where all lead-based paint was fully abated in a structure in accordance with the Michigan
Lead Abatement Act, being MCL 333.5451 et seq., and as certified by a certified lead inspector or
risk assessor, or where a certified lead inspector certifies that no lead paint exists on the rental
property, no further risk assessment or lead clearance shall be required in order to obtain a
Certificate of Compliance for structure.
(d) For purposes of this Section and any Subdivision thereof, the terms “abatement” or “abated”
means a measure or set of measures designed to permanently eliminate lead-based paint hazards
and includes:
    1. The removal of lead-based paint hazards and dust lead hazards, the permanent enclosure
    or encapsulation of lead-based paint, the replacement of lead-painted surfaces or fixtures, the
    removal or covering of soil lead hazards, and all preparation, cleanup, disposal, and post-
    abatement clearance testing activities associated with such measures, which shall be performed
    by a state certified lead abatement firm;
    2.     A project for which there is a written contract or other documentation which provides

    3.     A project resulting in the permanent elimination of lead-based paint hazards, conducted

    seq., except a project that is otherwise exempt under the Act;

   landscaping, or other activity, where the activity is not designed to permanently eliminate lead
   hazards, but is instead designed to repair, restore, or remodel a dwelling even though the
   activity may incidentally result in a reduction or elimination of a lead hazard, (b) an interim
