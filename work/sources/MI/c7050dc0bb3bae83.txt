The Delta County Finance Committee has been meeting regularly to discuss budget trends
and projections. Within those discussions, information has been conveyed about the
hardships that have been created due to the County's statutory Operating Millage, having
been established in 1976 with no updates since its inception, continuing to support the
general fund budget and the services that departments and agencies within the general
fund supply to the public.

That millage was set in 1976 at 5.4500 ($5.45 per $1,000 of taxable value). Due to the State
issued Headlee Rollback, that capture was most recently reduced to 4.9804 ($4.9804 per
$1,000 of taxable value). The Finance Committee is looking for discussion regarding asking
the voters of Delta County to approve removing the Rollback and placing the millage back
to its originally approved rate, which means an increase of .4696 mills ($.4696 per $1,000 of
taxable value), so that the County's general fund may capture the full millage approval rate
as established in 1976.

FACT SHEET – HEADLEE ROLLBACK AND HEADLEE OVERRIDE

INTRODUCTION
The term “Headlee Rollback” Became part of municipal finance in 1978 with the passage of the Headlee Amendment
to Michigan’s Constitution. In a nutshell, Headlee requires a local unit of government to reduce its millage when annual
growth on existing property is greater than the rate of inflation. As a consequence, the local unit’s millage rate gets
“rolled back” so that the resulting growth in property tax revenue, community-wide, is no more than the rate of inflation.
A “Headlee Override” is a vote by the electors to return the millage to the amount originally authorized via state
statute, and is necessary to counteract the effects of the “Headlee Rollback”.

IMPACT OF HEADLEE AMENDMENT
Since the passage of the Headlee Amendment, units of government are required to annually calculate a Headlee
rollback factor. The annual factor is then added to Headlee rollback factors determined in prior years resulting in a
cumulative Headlee rollback factor sometimes referred to as the “millage reduction fraction”. This total “millage
reduction fraction” is then applied to the millage originally authorized by state statute. In summary, the actual mills
available to be levied by a unit of local government is the product of the authorized millage rate times the total millage
reduction fraction. This is known as the “Headlee maximum allowable millage”.

IMPACT OF PROPOSAL A
Prior to Proposal A legislation passed in 1994, local governments were allowed to “roll up” their millage rates when
growth on existing property was less than inflation. “Roll ups” were a self-correcting mechanism that allowed local
governments to naturally recapture taxing authority lost due to Headlee rollbacks in prior years. A local government
could only “roll up” its millage rate to the amount authorized by state statute.

Additions to taxable value (such as a newly constructed property) are typically excluded from the Headlee Rollback
calculation. The 1994 General Property Tax Act changes did not specifically define “uncapped values” (increases
resulting primarily from property transfers) as exempt.

RESULT
Although it might appear that a community with an annual increase in uncapped property values would benefit
monetarily, uncapped values are treated as growth on existing property and trigger Headlee rollbacks. For local
governments levying at their Headlee maximum authorized millage, rolling back the maximum authorized millage rate
reduces the revenue that would have been generated from these increased property values. The increase in the taxable
value of property not transferred is capped at the lesser of inflation or five percent. Even though the taxable value of
a particular piece of property increases at the rate of inflation, the millage rate for the entire County is “rolled back”
as a result of the increase in the total taxable value of the County. The net result – a less than inflationary increase in
the actual dollars received from property taxes. Consequently, the 1994 change to the General Property Tax Act has
prevented local governments, like Delta County, from being able to share the benefits of any substantial market growth
in existing property values.

Delta County – Headlee Millage Override
Voters may be asked to return (override) the Delta County Operating
Millage rate from the current 4.9804 mils back to the statutory rate of
5.4500 millage rate.

What is Headlee?
Headlee is a 1978 Michigan constitutional amendment that requires local property tax rates to be
annually adjusted (rolled back) so they increase no more than the rate of inflation.

How does the Rollback Work?
Yearly, the State calculates a percentage that is used to require municipalities to reduce the local
property tax rate to conform with the rate of inflation for consumer goods.

How does the Rollback Affect the County?
Headlee reduces the County’s annual revenue growth, inhibiting how the County meets the normal
increased cost of doing the public’s business.

What is the Vote?
Voters would be asked to consider an override of the Headlee provisions to adjust the current millage
rate of 4.9804 mils back to the statutory approved rate of 5.4500.

Why is this happening now?
This is being proposed now, due to the reduction in tax revenue due to the Headlee Rollback, as seen
in fiscal year 2025. County expenditures have been steadily increasing and will outpace revenues.
They are projected to continue on this trajectory if the County maintains the same service levels and
does not find ways to increase its revenue.

How did we get here?
Costs have gone up, including employee pensions, labor contracts, fuel, building and maintenance
expenses, vehicles, insurance, technology, etc. The County has approached raising other revenue for
services, but would also like to remain competitive with neighboring counties.

How will Headlee affect my taxes?
The County would not be asking for a new tax. Instead, the County would be asking to raise the
Operational millage back up to the full capture that was established by State Statute back in 1976.
This would consist of an increase in .4696 mils or $.4696 per $1,000 of taxable property value.

              MIDC REGIONAL PUBLIC DEFENSE
                     Executive Summary for the
             Delta County Board of Commissioners
                                July 16, 2026
Michigan's public defense system has evolved significantly since the establishment
of the Michigan Indigent Defense Commission (MIDC). While the MIDC reforms
have strengthened constitutional representation and increased the quality of
services provided to indigent defendants, they have also created substantial
administrative, reporting, compliance, and staffing demands that are increasingly
difficult for rural counties to manage independently.

Recognizing these challenges, the MIDC has approved and supports the
development of a regional public defense model for Iron, Dickinson, and Delta
Counties. The proposed structure expands upon the successful nonprofit public
defense office currently operating in Iron County and creates a coordinated regional
system designed to reduce administrative overhead, improve services, stabilize
attorney recruitment, and provide long-term sustainability for public defense
throughout the central Upper Peninsula.

Under the model, each county will maintain a local public defense office staffed by
attorneys and support personnel assigned to serve that community and overflow
cases depending on need and availability. Existing assigned counsel relationships
will continue to play an important role during implementation and will remain
available for conflict cases, overflow assignments, and transition needs. This
approach preserves local relationships while creating a more stable and predictable
foundation for the future.

The regional structure allows participating counties to share resources that would
otherwise be difficult or impossible to maintain independently. All counties will
have access to staff investigator services, mitigation and case-management
professionals, centralized training, supervision, and recruiting efforts. Shared
mitigation services and coordinated recruitment initiatives have already
demonstrated success in neighboring Upper Peninsula communities, including
Marquette County, and provide a model for attracting and retaining attorneys who
are committed to living and working in the region.


The reality facing many rural counties is that the traditional assigned counsel
model is becoming increasingly difficult to sustain. Across the region, counties are
regularly forced to seek attorneys from outside their communities, and sometimes
from outside the Upper Peninsula entirely, to handle appointed cases. This
increases costs, creates scheduling challenges, reduces continuity of representation,
and does nothing to build long-term local capacity. The regional model focuses on
developing a sustainable pipeline of attorneys and support staff who live, work, and
remain in our communities.

The regional model also eliminates significant administrative duplication. Iron
County will serve as the administrative and compliance hub for the program,
handling MIDC reporting, financial management, invoicing, accounting, grant
administration, and compliance requirements on behalf of participating counties.
Dickinson and Delta Counties will contribute only their statutorily required local
share toward indigent defense services and will no longer need to maintain separate
financial reporting and accounting systems related to public defense operations.
Centralizing these functions will save substantial staff time, reduce administrative
costs, and ensure consistent compliance with MIDC requirements.

In addition to providing traditional criminal defense representation, the regional
office will offer comprehensive public defense services that reduce expenditures
currently incurred separately by counties. The office can provide coverage for
specialty courts, Friend of the Court proceedings, show-cause hearings, and other
court appearances that often require separate appointments or contracts under
existing systems. By consolidating these services under a single regional structure,
counties can achieve greater efficiency while expanding access to representation.

This initiative represents a collaborative regional solution to a regional challenge. It
preserves local offices, strengthens services, reduces administrative burdens,
enhances compliance, improves recruitment and retention, and positions Iron,
Dickinson, and Delta Counties for long-term success in meeting their constitutional
obligations while delivering high-quality public defense services to the residents
they serve.
