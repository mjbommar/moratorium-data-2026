THE CHARTER TOWNSHIP OF CALEDONIA ORDAINS:

        Section 1.      Temporary Moratorium for Certain Uses and Developments. The Zoning

Ordinance of the Charter Township of Caledonia is hereby amended by the amendment of Chapter

XXIII, entitled “Administration, Enforcement and Penalties;” by the addition of a new Section

23.11, to read in its entirety as follows:

        Section 23.11 MORATORIUM FOR CERTAIN USES AND DEVELOPMENTS.

             (a) Purpose. In response to increased proposals throughout Michigan for the siting of
                 new land uses, commonly referred to as Data Centers and Digital Mining, the
                 Township determines that it is necessary and in the best interest of the Township,
                 its residents and property owners to extend and enact a temporary moratorium on
                 certain types of uses and developments within the Township. The Township’s
                 Zoning Ordinance does not currently include a distinction between the smaller-
                 scale data and computer processing centers that are not new and the larger Data
                 Centers and Digital Mining Uses that are currently being proposed in the State and
                 does not include regulations that would assist in alleviating the potential negative


#3611587v1


                impacts of Data Centers and Digital Mining Uses. The Township desires to
                ascertain the most appropriate and safest means for regulating the use of lands,
                buildings and structures for Data Centers and Digital Mining Uses and to adopt
                possible amendments to the Zoning Ordinance and applicable regulatory
                ordinances to protect the health, safety and welfare of Township residents during
                the temporary moratorium.

             (b) Definitions. The following definitions apply to this section:

                        (1) Data Centers. Data centers are secure buildings filled with computer
                        servers that store, process and transmit data in support of internet functions
                        and other digital services, and which are typically accompanied by
                        networking equipment, water-fueled cooling systems (to prevent
                        overheating), backup power systems and security systems, and which
                        typically have minimal on-site staffing, limited customer visitation and no
                        retail activity.

                        (2) Digital Mining Uses. Digital mining, also commonly known as
                        cryptocurrency mining, is a use involving a building or structure that is
                        primarily occupied by computers used to mine digital currency, such as
                        Bitcoin or cryptocurrency.

             (c) Temporary Moratorium. A temporary moratorium on Township consideration
                 of applications for Data Centers and/or Digital Mining Uses is hereby adopted
                 under the following terms, conditions and limitations:

                        (1) During the temporary moratorium, no Township official, employee,
                        agent, contractor, board, agency or commission shall receive, consider,
                        review, approve, deny or hold public hearings on any zoning or building
                        applications for the use of any land, building or structure for a Data Center
                        and/or Digital Mining Use.

                        (2) The temporary moratorium is effective as to all applications for Data
                        Centers and/or Digital Mining Uses received in the Township on or after
                        the effective date of this section.

                        (3) The temporary moratorium shall remain in effect until February 17,
                        2027, unless previously terminated, extended or otherwise altered by the
                        Township Board. In addition, if the Township adopts amendments to the
                        Charter Township of Caledonia Zoning Ordinance addressing Data Centers
                        and/or Digital Mining Uses, the moratorium shall automatically terminate
                        upon the effective date of the amending ordinance, unless otherwise
                        extended by the Township Board.

                        (4) The provisions of this section automatically expire and have no further
                        effect after February 17, 2027, the date of expiration, unless altered by the
                        Township Board.

Cooling System: Any system, equipment, or apparatus used to regulate temperature and/or

and liquid cooling systems.

Data Center: A facility or operation consisting of computer systems and associated equipment
designed, constructed, and operated for the primary purpose of intake, processing, storage, and/or
distribution of computer data and electronic information. Associated equipment shall include all
buildings, structures, and components used in the operation of the Data Center, including but not

limited to servers, routers, switches, storage devices, cooling systems, backup power systems,

Sensitive Land Uses: A land use that includes educational facilities (Pre-K through 12), college

                                SECTION II
             ADD A NEW SECTION _________, TITLED “DATA CENTERS”

A new Section _____, titled “Data Centers” is hereby added to the Township Zoning Ordinance
to read as follows:

Section _______. Data Centers.

I.     PURPOSE

The purpose of this Section is to establish minimum requirements and regulations for the
construction, location, operation, and decommissioning of data centers. This Section is designed
to ensure that data centers are constructed and operated in a manner compatible with nearby uses
of land, the natural environment, and the capacities of public services and facilities. It is also
designed to mitigate nuisance impacts and to avoid adverse impacts on important areas such as
farmland, Sensitive Land Uses, or valuable natural resources.

II.    GENERAL STANDARDS

The following requirements shall apply to all Data Centers in addition to the other generally
applicable standards found in the Zoning Ordinance and to the extent of a conflict this Section
shall apply:

       A.      Site Selection. Data Centers shall only be allowed as a special land use in the
               Industrial District [customize to fit individual municipal industrial districts by
               reference].

       B.      Lot Coverage. Data Centers shall not exceed the maximum lot coverage allowed
               in the Industrial District.

       C.      Placement and Setbacks. Data Centers shall be arranged, designed, and
               constructed to be harmonious and compatible with the site and surrounding
               properties. Data Centers shall be sited to minimize the visual impacts of the bulk of
               the building on a line-of-sight basis from adjacent public streets and Sensitive Land
               Uses and to minimize the impacts to natural resources. Data Centers shall comply
               with the minimum setbacks applicable to the Industrial District except as otherwise
               provided in this Section.

               1.     Cooling System components including chillers, fans and compressors must
                                            2


                be surrounded by full height sound attenuation screening that must be
                designed to blend with the architectural style, materials, and color of the
                building:

           i.          If roof mounted, there must be a 1,500-foot minimum setback from
                       the sound attenuation screening to the closest Sensitive Land Use lot
                       lines. This distance is measured from the nearest part of the sound
                       attenuation screen to the property line of the Sensitive Land Use.

          ii.          If ground mounted, there must be a 1,000-foot minimum setback
                       from sound attenuation screening to the closest Sensitive Land Use
                       lot lines. This distance is measured from the nearest part of the sound
                       attenuation screen to the property line of the Sensitive Land Use .

     2.         Generators shall be located within full acoustic enclosures to the maximum
                extent possible. Enclosures must be designed to blend with the architectural
                style, materials, and color of the building and cannot be located within 1,000
                feet of any Sensitive Land Use, measured from the nearest part of the
                enclosure to the property line of the Sensitive Land Use . Roof mounted
                generators are prohibited.

D.   Height. The height of a Data Center, except for electric distribution and
     transmission poles, shall not exceed the maximum height permitted for structures
     in the Industrial District.

E.   Visual Screening. Views of a Data Center shall be minimized from public rights
     of way, adjacent properties, and Sensitive Land Uses, to the extent reasonably
     practicable using architectural features, earth berms, landscaping or other screening
     methods that will harmonize with the character of the property and surrounding
     area. The Planning Commission may require reasonable measures to minimize
     visual impacts by preserving existing natural vegetation, requiring new vegetative
     screening, or other appropriate measures on a site- specific basis. In making this
     determination, the Planning Commission is specifically authorized to consider
     whether additional visual screening measures are appropriate where a Data Center
     is proposed to be located on property adjoining or within the view of a Sensitive
     Land Use..

F.   Sound. The Data Center must comply with all federal and state regulations related
     to noise thresholds and any Zoning Ordinance Industrial District noise limitation.
     In addition, except as otherwise allowed in this Section, noise levels shall not
     exceed the following noise thresholds as measured at the Data Center property line
     shared with any non-industrial use or the property line of the closest Sensitive Land
     Uses as follows:

     1.         No sound shall exceed 55 dB(A) weighted (Laeq-10 min) between the
                daytime hours of 7am to 7pm.
                                          3


     2.     No sound shall exceed 45 dB(A) weighted (Laeq-10 min) between the
            nighttime hours of 7pm to 7am.

     3.     No source of steady tonal sound shall exceed 65 dB(C) weighted (Lceq-10
            min) at any time.

     4.     Octave Bands (dBz) will be maintained at acceptable levels.

G.   Lighting. The Data Center must implement dark sky-friendly lighting solutions,
     and all lighting must be designed to direct light downward. The facility must
     produce zero footcandles of light at the property line.

H.   Vibrations.

     1.     No Data Center shall be operated, maintained, or tested in a manner that
            generates a ground-borne vibration level exceeding a Peak Particle Velocity
            (PPV) of 0.01 inches per second (in/s) at any point along the Data Center's
            property line. This limit applies across all vibration frequencies between 1.0
            Hz and 100 Hz. The 0.01 in/s PPV threshold applies to measurements taken
            in any of the three mutually perpendicular axes (vertical, longitudinal, or
            transverse). If any single axis exceeds 0.01 in/s, the Data Center is in
            violation.

     2.     Generators must be equipped with vibration isolation and mitigation
            systems.

     3.     Data Centers must have continuous vibration monitoring at spacing of no
            less than 500 feet along all property lines which sit within 1,000 feet of the
            property line of any Sensitive Land Use.

I.   Water Usage Standards. A Data Center shall maintain a Water Usage
     Effectiveness of no more than two tenths (0.2). As used in this Section, “Water
     Usage Effectiveness” or “WUE” is defined as the ratio of total annual building
     water consumption (liters) to information technology equipment energy
     consumption (kilowatt-hour). Evaporative cooling is prohibited.

J.   Energy Usage.

     1.     Data Center facilities must be designed to maintain a Power Usage
            Effectiveness of no more than one and two-tenths (1.2). As used in this
            Section, “Power Usage Effectiveness” or “PUE” is defined as the ratio of
            total facility energy consumption divided by the total IT equipment energy
            consumption (servers, switches, storage devices, etc.). Total facility energy
            includes all power entering the building, such as cooling systems, lighting,
            power delivery components, and IT equipment. IT equipment energy

                                      4


                 includes only the power used by computers, servers, storage, and
                 networking devices.

     2.          Data centers must be designed and operated in compliance with the energy
                 code requirements specified in whichever of the following is most stringent:

            i.          The latest adopted Michigan Energy Code;

           ii.          The latest published ASHRAE Standard 90.4; or

          iii.          Michigan-specific data center energy code requirements adopted by
                        rule, which may include more detailed criteria.

     3.          The Data Center shall have on-site energy storage capable of supplying not
                 less than fifty percent (50%) of the facility’s peak electrical demand for a
                 minimum duration of fifteen (15) minutes, for purposes including grid
                 stabilization, brownout mitigation, and peak-load support. Energy storage
                 systems shall be configured to prioritize discharge during utility-declared
                 peak events and grid emergencies to reduce localized voltage sag,
                 transformer overload, and outage risk in surrounding neighborhoods.

     4.          The Data Center must demonstrate that it will supply the Center’s energy
                 needs independently of the current energy available within the Township
                 and that it complies with all state and federal requirements for the provision
                 of energy to supply its needs.

K.   Generators. Testing and exercising activities of generators are limited to 9am to
     5pm weekdays and are prohibited on any State holiday. No more than two (2)
     generators shall be tested simultaneously. The use of other backup power sources
     which serve to reduce or eliminate diesel generator noise and emissions shall be
     utilized to the maximum extent practicable. All generators must be U.S. EPA Tier
     4 compliant.

L.   General Impacts. Any use or activity producing air, dust, smoke, glare, exhaust,
     heat, or humidity in any form shall be carried on in such a manner that it is not
     perceptible at or beyond the Data Center property line.

M.   Public Safety. All Data Centers must develop and comply with an Emergency
     Response Plan and Fire Response Plan that shall provide reasonable protection of
     the public health, welfare and safety during all phases of construction and
     operation.

N.   Signage. The Data Center shall post all necessary signage to comply with any
     applicable electrical, building, and fire codes. Clearly visible warning signs shall
     be located at the base of any transformers, substations, or electrical components
     handling energy capable of causing harm to human beings. The Data Center
                                           5


     operation shall provide a 24-hour emergency contact signage visible at the access
     entrance.

O.   Access Drives. The Data Center shall provide safe and convenient vehicular access
     to the site, including sufficient on-site queuing areas at the security gate. The Data
     Center shall include access drives sufficient to accommodate adequate emergency
     response to all components.

P.   Surface Water Runoff. All surface water runoff created by construction and
     operation of the project shall be effectively managed on-site.

Q.   Building and Electrical Codes. The Data Center and all associated equipment
     shall comply with all applicable building and electrical code requirements and any
     applicable federal or state regulations. A qualified engineer shall provide written
     certification that the design, installation, and any interconnections are compliant
     with manufacturer and industry standards, all applicable local construction and
     electrical codes, and any applicable federal or state regulations.

R.   Permits. All Data Centers shall be required to apply for and obtain all necessary
     permits, including but not limited to building, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical
     permits.

      A.     All Data Centers shall be required to provide a Decommissioning Plan and comply

             3.         Define the conditions upon which decommissioning will be initiated. At a
                        minimum decommissioning shall be required if the Data Center is
                        abandoned. Abandoned shall mean that the Data Center ceases operations
                        for a period of 12 months, unless the current responsible party provides
                        sufficient evidence and guarantee of the intent to resume operations to the
                        Planning Commission.

             4.         State that decommissioning will allow the Data Center to be repurposed for
                        general industrial use through the removal of aspects of the Data Center
                        specific to use as a Data Center.

             5.         Describe the timeframe for completion of decommissioning activities;

             6.         Describe any agreement with the landowner regarding decommissioning;
                        and

             the applicant, operator, or party responsible for decommissioning and shall bind all

             shall be completed within 18 months.

   the coolant ever being exposed to the environment.

   § 16.55. Battery Storage, Data Center, and Digital Mining Uses.
   A. Purpose and Goals.
       1. The purpose of this Section is to establish standards for the siting, construction,
          operation and decommissioning of battery storage, data centers and digital mining uses.
          The goals of this Section are to:
           a. Protect residential and agricultural areas and land uses from potential adverse
              impacts of battery storage, data centers and digital mining uses;
           b. Direct these uses to industrial areas with adequate infrastructure, including
              electrical capacity, public water and public sewer;
           c. Protect groundwater, surface water and public and private water supplies from
              depletion and contamination;
           d. Minimize noise, vibration, glare, traffic and visual impacts on surrounding
              properties through performance standards, setbacks, buffering and screening;
           e. Protect the reliability and affordability of electrical service for other customers
              within the Township;
           f. Protect the public health and safety of the Township and its residents, including
              through fire protection and emergency planning standards;
           g. Ensure that sites are restored to a safe and usable condition upon discontinuation of
              the use through enforceable decommissioning requirements; and

                                             MEMORANDUM

To:            Caledonia Township Planning Commission
From:          Lynée Wells, AICP
Date:          September 14, 2026
Re:            Summary of Changes Since the August Meeting, and Comparison of the Draft Battery
               Storage, Data Center, and Digital Mining Ordinance (§ 16.55) to the Michigan Townships
               Association Model Data Center Ordinance, Version 1.0




This memo has three parts. Part I summarizes the substantive changes made to the draft ordinance since
the Planning Commission's August meeting. Part II and Part III compare the current draft — regulating
battery storage, data center, and digital mining uses (§ 16.55) — against the Michigan Townships
Association's (MTA) sample Data Center Ordinance, Version 1.0, identifying where the Township's draft
already exceeds the MTA model and where the MTA model offers provisions worth considering before
this draft advances further. Note that the draft dated 8.23.26 does not have any of the items from MTA at
this time as I am awaiting discussion with you at the September 21, 2026 meeting.

I. Summary of Changes Since the August Meeting

•   All three uses consolidated under a single Special Land Use review. This is the major structural
    change. As of the August meeting, the standards were split across two chapters: general standards in
    a new Chapter 3 section that applied automatically to any data center or digital mining use, and a
    separate Chapter 16 section requiring full Special Land Use (SLU) review only for medium-scale and
    hyperscale data centers and digital mining businesses. Small-scale data centers and anything below
    that threshold were never routed through SLU review at all. The current draft eliminates that split:
    every battery storage, data center, and digital mining use (other than a true accessory data center)
    now requires SLU approval under a single, unified § 16.55, reviewed against one consistent set of
    standards regardless of scale.

•   Battery storage added as a fully regulated, co-equal use. In August, battery storage appeared
    only once, as a passing reference under a safety subsection, with no definition, no setback, no
    insurance requirement, and no state-law carve-out. It's now defined and regulated on equal footing
    with data centers and digital mining throughout — title, definitions, SLU designation, setbacks, and
    insurance all treat it as a first-class use, including a new Public Act 233 disclaimer addressing the
    state's exclusive siting jurisdiction over large battery storage facilities.

•   Accessory data center threshold tightened. The exemption for small, building-housed data centers
    dropped from under 5 MW to under 1 MW — a meaningfully more conservative line for what can
    bypass SLU review entirely as merely “accessory.”

•   Setbacks became tiered by district and scale. The August draft used a flat setback (500 ft from
    any lot line, 1,000 ft from a residential structure). The current draft scales setbacks by both the


                                                                                                            1


    adjoining district (1,000 ft residential/agricultural, 750 ft other) and the facility's own size, with a
    reduced 500 ft carve-out for small-scale uses.

•   Liability insurance became tiered by scale. August required a flat $5 million/$5 million policy for
    every use regardless of size. The current draft scales coverage to $5M / $10M / $25M by facility tier,
    with materially higher coverage required for the largest facilities.

•   Fire-rated enclosure requirement strengthened. The required fire rating for equipment enclosures
    increased from 30 minutes to 3 hours.

•   Digital mining's fixed operating-hours restriction was dropped. August limited digital mining to
    9:00 a.m.–5:00 p.m., Monday through Friday — effectively incompatible with how mining operations
    run. The current draft has no operating-hours restriction on digital mining.

•   Dimensional standards adjusted. Minimum lot area increased from 40 to 60 acres; maximum
    single-building size decreased from 200,000 to 150,000 square feet; and a new 40% maximum lot
    coverage standard was added that did not exist in August. This is the same size as buildings at
    Patterson and 68th (the NE corner in CT; not Amazon in GT).

•   Decommissioning security review cycle tightened. The interval for reviewing and updating
    decommissioning financial assurance shortened from every 5 years to every 3 years.

•   Colocation accountability language expanded. August held the owner/operator generally
    responsible for lessee compliance. The current draft adds specific requirements: lease provisions
    binding every lessee to the ordinance, and language securing the Township's inspection access to
    leased space.

II. Where the Draft Ordinance Is Stronger Than the MTA Model

•   Scope covers three uses, not one. The draft regulates Battery Storage and Digital Mining as fully
    defined, co-equal uses alongside Data Centers (§ 16.55), including a Public Act 233 state-siting
    carve-out for large battery storage. The MTA model addresses only Data Centers — it has no battery
    storage framework, no digital-mining-specific standards, and no PA 233 disclaimer.

•   Colocation accountability. § 16.55(F)(22) requires colocation operators to bind every lessee to
    ordinance compliance by contract and to secure the Township's inspection access to leased space.
    The MTA model does not address colocation facilities at all.

•   Required liability insurance. § 16.55(F)(20) requires commercial general liability coverage tiered to
    facility scale ($5M / $10M / $25M), naming the Township as additional insured. The MTA model does
    not require the applicant to carry liability insurance.

•   Groundwater protection framework. § 16.55(G)(12)'s water feasibility study requires geologic
    mapping, a well inventory, and a long-term safe-yield determination, and flatly prohibits private wells
    for cooling or processing. The MTA model relies solely on a Water Usage Effectiveness ratio and
    proof of public water/sewer capacity — it has no groundwater protection framework.

•   Due-process protections on inspections. § 16.55(F)(19) builds in real procedural safeguards: 48-
    hour advance notice, inspection scope limited to the physical premises with customer and server data
    expressly off-limits, and an administrative-warrant fallback if consent is withdrawn. The MTA model
    has no comparable structured inspection process.

•   Proportionality limit on discretionary conditions. § 16.55(C) requires any Planning Commission-
    imposed condition to be supported by written findings and roughly proportional to an identified impact

                                                                                                               2


    — a real defense against an arbitrary-and-capricious challenge. The MTA model gives the Planning
    Commission broad, largely unconstrained discretion throughout.

•   Thermal / heat-island impact study. § 16.55(G)(3)(i) requires analysis of temperature changes
    within the site and a 1,000-foot buffer from the lot lines, resulting from waste heat. The MTA model
    has no equivalent — a real gap given documented local heat-island effects around large data centers.

•   Standalone hazardous materials standard. § 16.55(F)(12) sets specific operational standards —
    containment, setback, and screening — for hazardous materials storage and processing. The MTA
    model addresses hazardous materials only within the narrative Fire/Emergency Response Plan
    submittal, not as an independent performance standard.

III. Provisions to Consider Adopting or Adjusting

The MTA model does not cover battery storage or digital mining, so nothing below suggests narrowing
the draft's scope. Rather, these are areas where the MTA model's data center standard is more specific,
more numerically grounded, or addresses a risk the current draft does not yet reach.

•   Vibration standard. The MTA model has a concrete, ready-made standard: 0.01 in/s peak particle
    velocity across the 1–100 Hz range, measured on all three axes at the property line, with continuous
    monitoring every 500 feet within 1,000 feet of a Sensitive Land Use (§ II.H). The current draft has no
    measurable vibration standard. This is worth adopting directly — it resolves the open vibration
    question from our last conversation with an authoritative, sector-specific source rather than needing
    to choose between a human-comfort standard and a generic construction PPV number. This was also
    raised at the PC meeting by the public comment.

•   Noise measurement window and tonal cap. MTA measures Leq over 10-minute intervals (§ II.F)
    rather than the draft's one-hour average — by itself, a shorter window catches transient noise that an
    hourly average would smooth over. MTA also sets a distinct, quantified tonal-noise cap (65 dB(C)
    Lceq) using C-weighting, rather than leaving pure-tone noise to Planning Commission discretion as §
    16.55(F)(1)(d) currently does.

•   Setbacks tied to the sensitive use, not the zoning district. MTA's major setbacks — cooling
    equipment, generators — run from the Sensitive Land Use lot line itself (§ II.C), not the zoning district
    boundary, so a school or daycare sitting in an industrial-adjacent commercial district still gets full
    protection. The draft's setbacks (§ 16.55(F)(3)) currently key off residential/agricultural zoning districts
    only.

•   Roof-mounted vs. ground-mounted equipment. MTA sets a longer setback for roof-mounted
    cooling equipment (1,500 ft) than ground-mounted (1,000 ft) and bans roof-mounted generators
    outright, since rooftop noise and sightlines carry farther (§ II.C.1–2). The draft doesn't distinguish by
    mounting location.

•   Water and power efficiency metrics. MTA sets numeric performance standards — Water Usage
    Effectiveness ≤ 0.2 and Power Usage Effectiveness ≤ 1.2 — and flatly prohibits evaporative cooling
    with no exception (§ II.I–J). The draft requires closed-loop or recycled cooling but allows the Planning
    Commission to permit evaporative cooling towers by exception, and has no efficiency-ratio standard
    at all.

•   Grid-support energy storage mandate. MTA requires on-site storage capable of covering 50% of
    peak demand for 15 minutes, prioritized for discharge during utility-declared peak events (§ II.J.3).
    This is a genuinely novel requirement worth raising with the Township attorney before adopting — it's
    untested and may draw industry pushback — but it speaks directly to the grid-strain concerns that
    come up at every data center hearing. Note MTA § II.J.4 goes further and asks the facility to
                                                                                                                3


    demonstrate energy self-sufficiency “independently of the current energy available within the
    Township,” which appears to be in tension with the same model's own utility-capacity verification
    requirement — worth flagging to the attorney as a provision to sharpen or drop rather than adopt as
    written.

•   Ownership transfer notice. MTA requires 60 days' written notice to the township before any
    ownership transfer, with township approval confirming the new owner's capacity to perform (not
    unreasonably withheld) (§ II.T). The draft has no equivalent — a facility could change hands to an
    undercapitalized buyer with no Township visibility or recourse.

•   Decommissioning security cushion. MTA posts financial assurance at 125% of the estimated
    decommissioning cost — a real cushion above the bare estimate (§ IV.E). The draft (§ 16.55(F)(21))
    posts security at 100% of the third-party estimate. Worth considering the same buffer, though the
    draft's timing — security posted before any building permit issues — is earlier, and arguably stronger,
    than MTA's (posted at the start of full commercial operation).

•   Consolidated development agreement. MTA requires a single development agreement covering
    performance, maintenance, and security terms as one consolidated enforcement instrument (§ V).
    The draft achieves similar coverage through several separate escrow, security, and condition
    provisions scattered across § 16.55 — not wrong, just more fragmented. Worth discussing whether
    consolidating into one agreement would give the Township a cleaner single instrument to enforce
    against.

Summary

On balance, the Township's draft is the more comprehensive ordinance — it covers three distinct high-
impact uses where the MTA model covers one, and it includes several protections (liability insurance,
groundwater safeguards, inspection due process, proportionality limits on conditions) that the MTA model
omits entirely. The clearest near-term additions are the vibration standard and the sensitive-use-based
setback structure, both of which the MTA model supplies in ready-to-adapt form. The lighting, efficiency-
metric, and grid-support provisions are worth a substantive discussion with the Township attorney and
Planning Commission before deciding whether they fit Caledonia's circumstances, given they impose
more prescriptive operational and performance requirements than the draft currently contemplates.
