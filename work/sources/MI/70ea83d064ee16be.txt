of the R-2 district and is consistent with the established residential character planned for this
area. No conflicts with surrounding land uses were identified.

Environmental and physical site conditions, including soils associated with the
Carlisle-Houghton-Gilford complex, were reviewed and do not present significant constraints
that would be incompatible with the residential uses permitted in the requested district.
Additionally, evidence was provided showing that the property is not viable for development
under the existing B-2 zoning designation, and a residential use would be more consistent with
existing and emerging development patterns in the vicinity. A traffic comparison study has also
been submitted for review by the Township Engineer.

Low Density Residential

These areas are designated for single family residences, located between the rural residential and more urbanized
areas of the Township. This designation encompasses the majority of land planned for future residential use and
generally includes areas that do not have access to municipal water and sewers. Because public water and sewer
service is not planned to be expanded to these areas in the near future, the lot size proposed must be adequate to
accommodate onsite well and septic facilities. Many areas have already been developed where fewer
environmental constraints are found. However, the land immediately surrounding many of the lakes is designated
Jor residential land uses. These areas will need to be monitored to ensure the environmental integrity is maintained
and water quality remains satisfactory. Public sewer services have been extended to some of the low density
residential areas that surround School and Lyons Lakes, while the remaining lakes within this designation are
currently served by on-site.

Neighborhood Business

This designation is intended for a variety of small-scale retail and service businesses. This category defines local
business areas to concentrate commercial development within the defined nodes rather than scatter development
along the Old US-23 corridor. The uses in this designation are not intended to compete with larger shopping or

generally includes areas that do not have access to municipal water and sewers. Because public water and sewer
service is not planned to be expanded to these areas in the near future, the lot sizes proposed must be adequate to
accommodate onsite well and septic facilities. Many areas have already been developed where fewer environmental
constraints are found. However, the land immediately surrounding many of the lakes is designated for residential land
uses. These areas will need to be monitored to ensure the environmental integrity is maintained and water quality
remains satisfactory. Public sewer services have been extended to some of the low density residential areas that
surround School and Lyons Lakes, while the remaining lakes within this designation are currently served by on-site

facilities. The primary type of development within this classification is expected to be single-family residences on lots
that are roughly one acre in size.

Neighborhood Business

This designation is intended for a variety of small-scale retail and service businesses. This category defines local
business areas to concentrate commercial development within the defined nodes rather than scatter development
along the Old US-23 corridor. The uses in this designation are not intended to compete with larger shopping or
employment areas found in other communities that serve Township residents. Instead, they are primarily intended to
serve Township residents that do not wish to drive to the more distant commercial/office centers for their convenience
and daily shopping needs. Typical uses would include smaller general merchandising/retail establishments such as
convenience stores, banks, drycleaners, beauty/ barber shops, and small retail strips. Certain Neighborhood Business
nodes have been designated along Old US-23 in the areas north of Hyne, north of Hope Lake, and at the intersections
of Hilton Road and Spencer Road. This allows existing businesses to be recognized, while retaining much of the
northern corridor for residential development and open space. The Neighborhood Business designation will allow
convenience commercial uses to be provided to Township residents without intrusions into the residential
neighborhoods. The location of convenience commercial uses will provide traffic management benefits since most
patrons will already be on the road or traveling a short distance from nearby residential areas or from work. New
Neighborhood Business uses should develop within these nodes and within the mixed-use corridors, south of Hilton
and along Grand River Avenue. While there are some scattered commercial and office uses along the entire length of
the corridor, these may transition into other more residentially-oriented uses, with commercial uses locating closer to
the designated nodes.

A Conversation With Community Catalysts - Housing
Eileen Zilch, CEO and President- Community Catalysts (Howell)
Amy Johnston, Community Housing Director - Community Catalysts (Howell)

At a recent quarterly-held Livingston County Planner’s Luncheon event, The Community Catalysts (Howell) team of
Eileen Zilch and Amy Johnston conducted a presentation on a housing target market analysis that they performed for
Livingston County. Key highlights from the discussion included identifying existing housing gaps and exploring
strategies that local leaders can implement to enhance regional housing production.

The Housing Needs Analysis for Livingston County indicated that the county faces a significant and widening gap
between its existing housing supply and the number of units needed to support current and future residents. The
analysis estimates that Livingston County will require approximately 16,000 additional housing units by 2035. This
demand accounts for existing underproduction, households currently experiencing homelessness, anticipated
population growth, and the ongoing trend toward smaller household sizes, all of which increase overall unit demand.

Despite this need, recent housing production has averaged only about 500 units per year. If current trends
continue, the county is expected to fall short by approximately 11,000 units within the next decade, creating
upward pressure on prices and intensifying competition for available homes. To close this gap, housing production
would need to more than triple and be sustained at that elevated level for at least ten years.

The total housing need in 2035 is spread across income levels, represented as the percent of area median income
(AMI), as shown in Figure 2. The methodology section (part 3) of this memo describes the three components that sum
to total need, as well as the data sources used. Figure 3 translates AMI levels into nominal dollar ranges.

The vast majority of units are needed at the highest and lowest ends of the income spectrum, with over half of needed
housing units over 150% of AMI. It is common for jurisdictions to see need at both ends, but the high-income need in
Livingston County is highly skewed.

The analysis also highlighted a structural mismatch in the county’s housing market caused by years of
underproduction. Too few higher-cost units have resulted in higher-income households “renting down”—choosing
units below their income range due to limited availability. At the same time, the supply of lower-cost units remains
insufficient to meet the needs of households with more limited incomes. This dynamic pushes many lower-income
households into cost-burdened situations, spending more than 30 percent of their income on housing. As
higher-income households occupy lower-priced units, competition for those units increases, constraining options for
lower-income households and exacerbating affordability pressures.

The cumulative effect of these trends is a tight housing market characterized by scarcity across multiple price points,
with the most severe consequences felt by lower-income residents. Without substantial increases in production,
Livingston County is likely to see rising rates of cost burden, limited housing choices for residents across the income
spectrum, and continued displacement pressure on vulnerable households.

DISCUSSION
The rezoning request was reviewed based on the review considerations listed in Section 23-10 of
the Zoning Ordinance and the Charter Township of Brighton Master Plan.

   1. Consistency with the goals, policies and future land use map of the Brighton Township
      Master Plan including any sub area or corridor studies. If conditions have changed
      since the Master Plan was adopted, the consistency with recent development trends in
      the area.
       This project is part of the Low-Density Residential designation except for the lot on S.
       Old US 23 which is part of the neighborhood business designation. The Low-Density
       Residential Designation is designated for single-family residences, located between the
       rural residential and more urbanized areas of the Township. This designation
       encompasses the majority of land planned for future residential use, and generally
       includes areas that do not have access to municipal water and sewers. Because public
       water and sewer service is not planned to be expanded to these areas in the near future,
       the lot sizes proposed must be adequate to accommodate on-site well and septic facilities.
       Many areas have already been developed where fewer environmental constraints are
       found. However, the land immediately surrounding many of the lakes is designated for
       residential land uses. These areas will need to be monitored to ensure the environmental
       integrity is maintained and water quality remains satisfactory. Public sewer services have
       been extended to some of the low-density residential areas that surround School and
       Lyons Lakes, while the remaining lakes within this designation are currently served by
       on-site facilities. The primary type of development within this classification is expected
       to be single-family residences on lots that are roughly one acre in size. The proposed
       development meets the size requirements for the R-2 zoning district.

   2. Compatibility of the site's physical, geological, hydrological and other environmental
      features with the potential uses permitted in the proposed zoning district.
      The soils in the area are part of the Carlisle-Houghton-Gilford Association which includes
      nearly level, very poorly drained, organic soils and moderately coarse textured soils on
      outwash plains in glacial drainageways, and on lake plains.

   3. Compatibility of all the potential uses allowed in the proposed zoning district with
      surrounding uses and zoning in terms of land suitability, impacts on the environment,
      density, nature of use, traffic impacts, aesthetics, infrastructure and potential
      influence on property values.
      Evidence was submitted which states that the site cannot develop under the current B-2

   4. The capacity of Township infrastructure, utilities and services is sufficient to

      The site will have well and sewer and the sewer is capable of handling the permitted request.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The purpose of this report is to evaluate tertiary filtration alternatives to replace the existing continuous
backwash sand filters at the Charter Township of Brighton Wastewater Treatment Plant (WWTP). The
existing tertiary filtration system has reached the end of its useful life and filtration performance has
declined. This has led to increased maintenance and operational issues with the filtration system. The
existing filtration system was constructed in 2000 with a peak hydraulic capacity of 2.10 MGD.

Factors considered within the evaluation include capital expenses, constructability, operation, maintenance,
and repair costs, impact on WWTP hydraulics, and capacity for future expansion. This evaluation was
completed with the use of historic operating data, including flow records and wastewater characterization,
and applicable design standards provided through the Ten States Standards for Wastewater Systems.

Four different alternatives were considered for replacement of the filtration system:

         Alternative 1: Continuous Backwash Sand Filtration
         Alternative 2: Horizontal Cloth Disk Filters
         Alternative 3: Vertical Cloth Disk Filters
         Alternative 4: Membrane Filtration

Alternative 2 – Replacement with Horizontal Disk Filters was selected as the recommended alternative. At
an estimated capital cost of $2,170,000 it has the second lowest NPW of principal alternatives developed,
while improving reliability, providing facilities capable of delivering continued compliance with regulatory
and permit requirements, and limiting the environmental impact of WWTP operations and discharge. The
layout of this alternative will provide capacity for future expansion, while improving filter accessibility and
ease of maintenance. It was further determined to be the greatest “plug and play” option, therefore limiting
structural changes and construction downtime.

Alternative 1 (replacement in kind) carries the lowest net present worth but was not recommended because
the existing continuous-backwash sand filter has documented reliability and media-degradation problems
that this alternative would carry forward.

INTRODUCTION
The purpose of this feasibility study is to identify and evaluate alternatives for the replacement of the tertiary
filtration process at the Charter Township of Brighton Wastewater Treatment Plant (WWTP).

This study includes an overview of the existing filtration system and an analysis of the advantages and
disadvantages of each alternative. Project goals were developed to evaluate if each alternative would
address the basic needs of the project before being analyzed further. Each capable alternative would then
be analyzed based on the capital expenses, operations and maintenance costs, impact on hydraulic
capacity, process redundancy and system performance. The economic analysis of each alternative
considers the constructability of the alternatives and the necessary modifications to existing infrastructure
needed to install the system.

Project Background
The WWTP was constructed in 2000 with the following treatment processes:

         Fine Screening
         Activated Sludge Secondary Treatment
         Final Clarification
         Tertiary Filters
         Ultraviolet Disinfection
         Cascade Aeration
         Aerated Storage of Biosolids

The WWTP upgraded the UV disinfection equipment in 2025 to replace aging equipment and improve
hydraulic conditions through the process.

Wastewater Flows
Treated effluent is discharged to Woodruff Creek in accordance with NPDES Permit No. MI0054968. The
WWTP is designed to treat an average daily flow of 0.65 MGD and a peak hydraulic flow of 2.1 MGD. The
daily flow received by the plant between 2022 and 2026 averaged 0.32 MGD. A summary of the plant’s
wastewater flows are shown in Table 1.

Wastewater Quality
Over the past three years the WWTP has recorded several total phosphorus exceedances. Because the
effluent limit is for total phosphorus — which includes dissolved phosphorus that tertiary filtration cannot
remove without upstream coagulation — filtration is not expected to completely resolve these exceedances,
and phosphorus compliance is outside the scope of this evaluation.

EXISTING FILTRATION SYSTEM
Tertiary filtration is the final stage of the wastewater treatment process, prior to disinfection, occurring after
secondary biological treatment has already removed the bulk of solids and organic material from the
wastewater. Its purpose is to polish the treated water by capturing fine suspended solids, residual biological
floc, and other particulates that pass through earlier treatment stages. This is a critical step because these
remaining particles can shield pathogens, such as bacteria and viruses, from the ultraviolet (UV) disinfection
system located downstream, reducing its effectiveness. Reliable tertiary filtration is also a regulatory
requirement — the facility's discharge permit establishes limits on total suspended solids (TSS), a measure
of the particulate matter remaining in the treated water, to protect the quality of the receiving water body.
Consistently meeting these permit limits depends on the filtration system performing effectively upstream
of disinfection.

System Configuration
The WWTP currently utilizes a continuous up-flow sand filtration system, which was installed at the WWTP
in 2000. This system was designed for an average day flow (ADF) of 0.65 million gallons per day (MGD), a
maximum day flow of 1.0 MGD, and a peak hourly flow of 2.1 MGD.

The system consists of 8 filters with a combined area of 400 square feet. Additionally, the system includes
influent, drain and reject piping and valves to each filter. A reject pump station is located adjacent to the
filter building to pump filter drain and reject water back to the secondary treatment process. The designed
continuous backwash increases hydraulic loading to the secondary treatment process which could be
improved with alternative filtration processes.

At the designed filter area with eight filters in service, the filtration rate at peak hour flow is 3.64 gallons per
minute per square foot. It is recommended by Ten States Standards for Wastewater Systems that the
maximum filtration rate be 5.0 gallons per minute per square foot. To maintain the maximum filtration rate
below 5.0 gallons per minute per square foot, a minimum of six of the eight filters need to be online at peak
hour flow.

System Condition
As the system has aged, the filtration media has degraded, leading to poor filtration, backwashing
inefficiencies, and plugging of media that has resulted in the removal of multiple filter basins from service
on a permanent or temporary basis.

This has resulted in reduced reliability and redundancy of the existing filter system while increasing the
need for maintenance.

Hydraulic Capacity
The filters were designed to pass 2.1 MGD on a combined basis and will achieve the recommended filtration
rate of 5 gallons per minute per square foot with a maximum of 2 units out of service. A hydraulic analysis
of the tertiary filtration system was performed considering the final clarifier piping, UV disinfection piping
and effluent conditions. Based on the model created, the tertiary filters are not a limiting hydraulic factor
and there is adequate head available through the process to accommodate the primary alternatives
developed in subsequent sections.

This section describes the tertiary filtration alternatives selected for evaluation at the Brighton Township
WWTP. The alternatives were developed based on their ability to meet the needs of the Township while
remaining within financial, regulatory, and technical constraints.

Alternative 1 – Continuous Backwash Sand Filtration
General
Alternative 1 includes replacement in kind of the existing continuous backwash sand filtration system and
includes replacement of the equipment and filtration media and equipment located in the eight concrete
basins. A new compressed air system would be installed to facilitate air-lift of the sand media, and will
include a new compressor, air piping, and controls. New grating will also be installed above each basin.

Operational Description
The continuous backwash sand filtration system utilizes eight basins filled with sand filtration media. Filter
influent is routed to the bottom of the basin and percolates up through the sand media. As the water rises,
the solids become entrapped within the void space of the media. After passing through the full sand column,
final effluent discharges over the top of the weir into the common effluent channel.

As solids accumulate within the media void space, headloss through the filter gradually increases, reducing
filtration capacity. To prevent excessive loss, the sand media continuously backwashes. An air-lift manifold
centrally located at the conical tank bottom transfers solids-laden sand up the backwash tube. At the top of
the tube, a corrugated outlet facilitates sand washing, as the heavy sand can drop out of the outlet, back to
the top of the sand bed, while lighter solids stay suspended and flow over the reject weir. The average
backwash water flow rate returned to the secondary process is not anticipated to change for Alternative 1.

Alternative 2 – Horizontal Cloth Disk Filters
General
Alternative 2 includes removal of the existing continuous backwash sand filters and replacement with
horizontally oriented cloth disk filter units. Three filter units, each constructed with three separately
isolatable disks and an outboard backwash pump, will be installed, occupying three of the existing eight
filter basins. As configured, two units provide the capacity to handle peak flow of 2.1 MGD, providing 50%
redundant capacity with the third unit.

Operational Description
The cloth disk filters utilize a series of hollow disks, wrapped in a pile-cloth media casing. The disk structure
allows for outside-in flow, where flow travels through the cloth media and filtrate is collected within the
interior disk void space. Each disk has an individual filtrate pipe, which routes filtrate discharge into the
existing effluent channel. Each disk may be individually isolated through the closure of a gate valve located
on each filtrate pipe. Suspended solids are captured on the outside of the cloth media, allowing for effective
removal through the use of backwash equipment.

Unlike the existing sand filtration process, which utilizes continuous backwashing, the cloth disk filters utilize
intermittent backwashing. The backwash system uses paired suction headers on each side of the disk,
which are connected to dedicated backwash pumps. As solids accumulate on the media, pore space is
consumed, which increases headloss through the filter. When headloss causes the basin water level to
exceed an operator-adjustable level, a backwash cycle is initiated. At that point, a dry-pit submersible pump




877220_AlternativesReport_Final.docx


                       Charter Township of Brighton | Tertiary Filtration Alternatives Analysis Report | July 2026
                                                                                                           Page 6

is called to run a timed backwash cycle before returning the disk to normal operation. If the basin water
level does not reach the backwash setpoint within a preset period, an automatic, routine backwash cycle is
initiated to provide backwashing during periods of low solids loading. The average backwash water flow
rate returned to the secondary process is estimated to be 250 gpm for Alternative 2.

A drain at the bottom of the tank allows for further removal of solids that settle within the filter basin. The
drain will discharge out the side of the tank and connect to a new, common gravity drain which will discharge
to the existing reject pump station and back to the head of the secondary process.

Alternative 2 provides approximately 500 square feet of filter surface, which equates to 167 square feet of
filter surface per basin. With one basin out of service, this provides approximately 335 square feet of
filtration surface, resulting in a hydraulic loading of 4.36 gallons per minute per square foot at the rated peak
hour flow of 2.1 MGD.

Alternative 3 – Vertical Cloth Disk Filters
General
Alternative 3 includes replacement of the existing continuous backwash sand filters with vertically oriented
cloth disk filters. Two filter units, each constructed with six disks and an outboard backwash pump, will be
provided. Each filter unit is designed for a peak flow capacity of 2.1 MGD, providing 100% redundant
capacity.

Operational Description
As with the horizontal system, the cloth disk filters utilize a series of hollow disks, wrapped in a pile-cloth
media. The disk structure allows for outside-in flow, in which flow travels through the cloth media and filtrate
is collected within the interior disk void space.

However, unlike the horizontal configuration, the vertical system features a single filtrate pipe, which the
disks are concentrically stacked upon. The common filtrate pipe discharges into the existing effluent
channel. Because the disks are hydraulically connected to a single filtrate pipe, they cannot be individually
isolated. As such each basin will need to be taken offline to facilitate cleaning and replacement of cloth
media.

As with the horizontal configuration, solids are captured on the outside of the cloth media, allowing for
effective removal using backwash equipment, which utilizes a pair of suction headers that surround each
side of the disk with interconnecting piping to designated backwash pumps. The backwash cycle for the
vertical cloth filter is conducted in the same way that the horizontal filter operates, where backwashing is
first initiated in response to rising water level in the basin due to headloss increasing through the filter. If
the headloss does not increase and initiate backwashing within a preset period of time, a backwash cycle
is initiated to provide routine backwashing during periods of low solids loading. The average backwash
water flow rate returned to the secondary process is estimated to be 250 gpm for Alternative 3.

A drain at the bottom of the tank allows for further removal of solids that settle within the filter basin. The
drain will discharge out the side of the tank and connect to a new, common gravity drain which will discharge
to the existing reject pump station.

Alternative 3 provides approximately 646 square feet of filter surface, which equates to 323 square feet of
filter surface per basin. With one basin out of service, this provides approximately 323 square feet of
filtration surface, resulting in a hydraulic loading of 4.51 gallons per minute per square foot at the rated peak
hour flow of 2.1 MGD.

Alternative 4 – Membrane Filtration
General
Alternative 4 includes removal of the existing continuous backwash sand filters and replacement with a
membrane filtration system, new membrane units, filtrate pumps, controls, air/water scour system, and
backwash pumps and piping. To facilitate installation of the new equipment, significant structural
modifications would be required within the filtration building.

Operational Description
Membrane filtration uses membranes with fine pores to separate solids from water. In a typical outside-in
flow configuration, solids are retained on the membrane exterior surface while filtrate passes through the
membrane and is collected for discharge downstream. Continuous crossflow, coupled with sequenced air
scouring and wash cycles are utilized to limit solids accumulation at the membrane interface. However, the
membranes do gradually foul, and routine takedown and cleaning of membranes is required. Membrane
replacement is expensive and adds to the long-term maintenance cost of the system. Book life on the
membranes is generally expected to be 7 years.

Rejected solids accumulate within the membrane tank, gradually increasing the TSS of the basin. To
manage solids accumulation, a recycle pump is utilized, which will pump a portion of the filter volume back
upstream to the secondary process. Backwash water flow rate returned to the secondary process is highly
variable for membrane processes, estimated to exceed the backwash volumes necessary for Alternatives
1, 2, and 3.

Membrane filtration processes typically have high relative energy demands to pump filtrate through the pore
space in membranes. Coupled with high maintenance demands for cleaning and replacing membrane units,
this alternative is not anticipated to be cost effective to operate, and as such, was determined to not meet
project objectives. In addition to high maintenance demands regarding cleaning and replacement of
membrane units, the time required for these activities is expected to significantly exceed that of Alternatives
2 and 3. Alternative 4 is not considered to be a viable alternative and will not be considered for further
evaluation.

ALTERNATIVES ANALYSIS
Analysis of the viable alternatives, Alternatives 1, 2 and 3, involved comparison of the technologies
considered with regard to performance, constructability, maintenance, reliability, and cost. The following
sections discuss the benefits, limitations, and cost consideration for the filtration system alternatives.

Performance
The alternatives were evaluated against the most stringent permitted effluent TSS limit — the 22 mg/L
monthly average that applies May through September (30 mg/L applies October through April), with
corresponding 7-day maximums of 33 and 45 mg/L. Designing to the summer limit governs filter sizing.

Though each alternative was determined to be capable of achieving the permitted effluent solids limit, there
are known issues with the existing sand filters resulting in the potential for excessive solids pass through
and filter unavailability that has resulted in excessive effluent solids. These are discussed in greater detail
in the Maintenance and Reliability section.

Constructability
Alternative 1 – Continuous Backwash Sand Filtration System
Given that the existing system is sized to maintain a filtration rate below 5.0 gallons per minute per square
foot with two filter units out of service, it is proposed that this alternative would utilize a phased approach,
where two filters, including the internal equipment and media would be demolished and rebuilt concurrently.
Following this rebuild, the new filters would be placed in service, and an additional two filters, including
media and internal equipment, would be replaced. This replacement method would be employed to replace
each of the eight filter units in the system, while maintaining six units in service throughout construction.

Alternative 2 – Horizontal Cloth Disk Filter
The proposed system includes 9 disks, configured as three, 3-disk units. Each unit is proposed to be
installed within one of the existing filter basins, such that only three of the existing eight basins are required
to construct this alternative. To facilitate the installation of each filter unit, three of the existing sand filter
basins will have to be demolished. Demolition will include complete removal of all sand media and
equipment, though the concrete structure of the basin will remain. Once demolition is complete, grout fill
would be placed within the basins to raise the floor elevation and provide a slight slope to a central
underdrain. A series of stainless-steel rails would be installed to suspend each filter unit new drain and
backwash piping would be installed.

During construction, the five remaining sand filters may be left in operation to limit filter downtime. Once
constructed, the disk filters would be placed into service, and the remaining filters would be demolished.
Given the minimal operational downtime and structural changes required, as well as the preservation of
adequate space and capacity for future treatment expansion, Alternative #2 is viewed as the most practical
implementation approach.

Alternative 3 – Vertical Cloth Disk Filter
The proposed system is composed of two, 6-disk units. Each unit is proposed to be installed within a space
currently occupied by two of the existing filter basins. As each unit would occupy the equivalent space of
nearly one existing basin, it is proposed that intermediate walls between two adjacent basins be removed
to provide room for installation and maintenance activities. This approach will consume the space currently
occupied by four of the existing eight filter basins, leaving four of the original eight basins available for future
use or expansion.

To facilitate construction of this alternative, it is anticipated that a two-phased approach would be used. In
so doing, two existing filters would be demolished concurrently and the common wall between them would
be removed. A grout fill would then be placed within the basin to raise the floor elevation and provide a
slight slope to a central underdrain. At this point, the filter equipment would be installed along with new
underdrain and backwash piping, with wall penetrations cored accordingly. Following completion and start-
up of one new filter unit, demolition and ensuing construction for the remaining unit would begin.

As with Alternative 2, some of the sand filter basins may be able to be left in operation during construction.
However, the necessity to sawcut walls and implement other significant structural modifications may
significantly reduce the ability to keep existing basins in operation.

Maintenance and Reliability
Alternative 1 – Continuous Backwash Sand Filtration System
The existing sand filtration system has several known issues, primarily involving media degradation and
media plugging.

As the filter media ages, the sand particles gradually break down, resulting in smaller and non-uniform void
spacing between particles. The non-uniformity in void spacing, coupled with the nature of fluidized beds,
can result in excessive solids passing through the system.

As void spacing continues to decrease, solids often blind-over the media near the point of entrance to the
filter. These biologic solids and fines from the sand degradation process can often cause agglomeration of
media, resulting in large, solidified masses that that are not effectively processed through the air-lift cleaning
process.
This eventually causes large scale media plugging, and water is unable to flow through the filter. As a result,
the filter must be taken offline for media cleaning and eventual media replacement as the particles continue
to degrade.

Another issue regarding media degradation is that the sand particles eventually become light enough that
they do not settle out of the cleaning structure as they are designed and may carry over the reject weir with




877220_AlternativesReport_Final.docx


                       Charter Township of Brighton | Tertiary Filtration Alternatives Analysis Report | July 2026
                                                                                                           Page 9

backwashed solids. This presents long-term maintenance issues, as this sand flows to the reject pump
station and oxidation ditch causing wear on the pumps and accumulating in the ditches.

Alternative 2 – Horizontal Cloth Disk Filter
Alternative 2 has relatively low maintenance needs, with the primary maintenance efforts being, manual
cleaning or replacement of the filter cloths, as they gradually become impacted by solids and normal wear.

To facilitate media cleaning, each disk may be individually isolated, allowing other disks to remain in service
through the cleaning process. Each disk is equipped with a lift ring, and an overhead hoist will be provided
that can be used to lift the disk from the filter basin. Once removed from the basin, the media casing can
be pulled from the internal disk structure and washed with dish soap or citric acid cleaning solution. Once
cleaned, the media casing is fastened back to the disk, and the disk can be placed back in service. Typically,
this cleaning should be conducted when there is a noticeable increase beyond 1.5 to 2 times the average
number of backwashing frequencies. Best practice is to ensure that the filters are cleaned one to two times
per year.

In the event that the media needs to be replaced, it can be readily discarded with existing trash service at
the WWTP. In general, the media typically needs to be replaced every five years, though this highly
dependent the quality of the influent to the system. High influent solids loading, particularly solids rich in
fats, oil, and grease will cause media to degrade relatively quick, where low suspended solids loading and
routine cleaning can prolong the media life beyond ten years of operation.

Other maintenance activities are largely limited to routine inspections and replacement of backwash pump
wear parts.

Regarding reliability, the system is sized to provide 50% redundant capacity, meaning that an entire basin
can be taken out of service and the system still has capacity to handle peak hour flows. It is anticipated that
the backwash pumps will be connected to a common suction header, with proper valving, to facilitate
backwashing with redundant pumps, should a one be out of service.

Additionally, this layout only utilizes three of eight existing filter basins, leaving five basins available for
future expansion.

Alternative 3 – Vertical Cloth Disk Filter
Alternative 3 offers improved ease of maintenance relative to the existing sand filters, though not to the
level that the horizontal cloth disk filter system offers.

The vertical orientation allows for treatment capacity to be concentrated on a small area, by stacking
multiple disks vertically upon the same footprint. However, by stacking the disks, they cannot be individually
isolated and removed for maintenance. As such, each basin must be individually isolated to facilitate
cleaning.

Once isolated and drained using the underdrain facilities, the operator may enter the basin to remove the
disks. Each disk is constructed of segments, which are individually wrapped with the cloth media. These
segments are removed from the filter manifold individually, and the media wrap can be removed and
cleaned with either dish soap or citric acid cleaning solution. Once the media wrap has been cleaned, it can
be fastened back to the supporting frame and reinstalled to the filter manifold.

Since this cleaning process is anticipated to require more time than the horizontal configuration and overall
effort, it is anticipated to have a larger annual Operation, Maintenance, and Replacement (OM&R) budget.
As mentioned, cleaning and maintenance activities require the respective basin to be taken offline. As such,
the system is designed to provide 100% redundancy. This means that each basin provides the necessary
capacity to process peak hour flow with the other basin offline.

Given that this approach only utilizes four of the eight existing basins, it provides ample capacity to install
two more units in future expansions, tripling firm capacity at full buildout.

Monetary Evaluation
Financial analysis of the alternatives was conducted using a net present worth (NPW) methodology, in
which capital, operation, maintenance, and replacement, and salvage value costs were estimated
separately and discounted to present value over the 20-year planning period. This analysis provides a
consistent basis for comparing alternatives by accounting for the time value of money. The net present
worth of each alternative is calculated as the sum of the initial capital cost and the present worth of
operation, maintenance, and replacement costs, less the present worth of the estimated salvage value at
the end of the planning period.

Salvage value is calculated at the end of a 20-year analysis period, during which portions of proposed
structures or equipment may retain residual value. Salvage value is determined in accordance with industry
standards using straight-line depreciation and estimated useful lives for affected assets. In general,
concrete structures, earthwork basins, and piping have useful lives of 30 to 50 years, while equipment has
a useful life of 10 to 20 years.

The cost of labor, equipment, and materials is not escalated over the planning period since it assumes an
increase in these costs will apply equally to each alternative. The interest charge during construction
(capitalized interest) would not significantly influence the comparison of alternatives and was not included
in the cost-effective analysis.

The following cost comparison details were specifically addressed and were applied in the present worth
analysis.

         Capital costs were included for each identified improvement.
         Sunk costs were excluded from the present worth cost. Sunk costs for the project include existing
          land, existing waterworks facilities, and outstanding bond indebtedness.
         Operation, Maintenance, and Replacement, (OM&R) costs were included in the present worth cost.
         The economic comparison is based on a 20-year planning period and a discount rate of 2.0%, per
          OMB Circular No. A-94 for FY2027.
         Salvage values were included in the present worth cost.
         Energy costs escalation were assumed equal between the alternatives and therefore are not
          adjusted over the 20-year period.
         Land purchase/acquisition costs were not applicable to the principal alternatives.
         Mitigation costs are included in the Project Costs and considered in the present worth cost.

RECOMMENDED ALTERNATIVE
In determining the recommended alternative, each principal alternative was evaluated against the project
goals, stated above. They were also compared against each other regarding performance, constructability,
maintenance, reliability, and net present worth.

Each viable alternative was determined to be capable of providing continued compliance with regulatory
and permit requirements, as well as limiting the environmental impact of the WWTP operations and
discharge.

Alternative #1 - Continuous Backwash Sand Filtration System, provides the lowest NPW, but there are
considerable concerns regarding the ability to provide reliable service, given the observed operational
problems of the existing system.

Alternative #3 – Vertical Cloth Disk Filters, has reduced long-term maintenance and reliability concerns,
though it does require taking parallel basins offline to facilitate maintenance activities. Given the moderate
maintenance requirements and opinion of probable capital cost, this Alternative was determined to have
the highest NPW of the three principal alternatives.

Alternative #2 provides the second lowest NPW but provides significant value in minimizing direct labor due
to overall ease of maintenance, therefore providing the lowest NPW of OM&R cost over the 20-year
planning period. Alternative #2 also provides a readily constructable project with adequate capacity for
future expansion. As such, Alternative 2 – Horizontal Cloth Disk Filter is the recommended alternative.

                  AN ORDINANCE TO ENACT A TEMPORARY MORATORIUM ON
                                    DATA CENTERS
      An Ordinance to protect the public health, safety, and welfare by establishing regulations relating to a
limited moratorium on considering applications for data centers while the Township completes a study of
ordinances and regulations pertaining thereto; to provide for severability; to repeal all ordinances or parts of
ordinances in conflict therewith; and to provide an effective date.


                                 CHARTER TOWNSHIP OF BRIGHTON

                                  LIVINGSTON COUNTY, MICHIGAN
                                                 ORDAINS:

                                                 SECTION I
                                                 FINDINGS

       The Board of Trustees for the Charter Township of Brighton has determined the following:

       1. That the approval of data centers may result in or produce negative impacts on permitted land uses
          and development and may harm the public health, safety, and welfare of property owners and residents
          of the Township.

       2. The Township Board is aware of significant public health, safety, and welfare concerns being raised
          by the public about the impacts of data centers.
       3. The Township has a legitimate public purpose in assessing the regulation of the establishment and use
          of data centers within the Township to ensure that data centers do not interfere with other land uses,
          or have substantial negative impacts on the environment, public health, and safety.
       4. The Township Board finds that adopting a temporary moratorium is reasonable and necessary for,
          among other reasons, the following reasons:

           A. Michigan courts have recognized that a moratorium is a common and legitimate tool to
              preserve the status quo while formulating a development strategy.

           B. The Township Board desires to review and study the public health, safety, and welfare
              concerns regarding data centers and any Township regulations that may impact and/or
              regulate such projects.

           C. The adoption of a temporary moratorium will allow the Township Board adequate time to
              study and possibly implement revisions to the Township’s Ordinances and regulations,
              including attention to and consideration of citizen input and involvement, public debate,
              and full consideration of all issues and points of view.


       5. The Township Board accordingly determines that it is desirable and in the public interest for the
          reasons set forth above that the Township Board adopt a temporary moratorium on the acceptance
          and/or processing of any applications for data centers.

                                          SECTION II
                                MORATORIUM/TERM OF MORATORIUM

        The Township Board adopts a temporary moratorium on the acceptance and/or processing of any
applications for a data center. The temporary moratorium is in effect for six (6) months following the adoption of
this ordinance by the Township Board, or until the effective date of any amended or new Township Ordinances
or regulations addressing data centers are effective, whichever date occurs first.

                                             SECTION III
                                      EXTENSION OF MORATORIUM

       Before this moratorium expires, the Township Board may extend the temporary moratorium by resolution
as appropriate to allow sufficient time to complete amendments or additions to its Ordinances and regulations.

                                                 SECTION IV
                                                SEVERABILITY

        The provisions of this Ordinance are hereby declared to be severable. If any clause, sentence, word, section
or provision is hereafter declared void or unenforceable for any reason by a court of competent jurisdiction, it
shall not affect the remainder of such Ordinance which shall continue in full force and effect.

                                                  SECTION V
                                                   REPEAL

       All Ordinance or parts of Ordinances in conflict herewith are hereby repealed.


                                                SECTION VI
                                              EFFECTIVE DATE

       This Ordinance shall take effect upon publication after adoption.

AGENDA TOPIC: State of Michigan Fire Equipment Grant Program

EXPLANATION OF TOPIC:

Brighton Area Fire Authority (BAFA) reached out to the Township and inquired if the Township
would be willing to submit a Fire Equipment Grant application. The State of Michigan, under
section 991(2) of Public Act 22 of 2025, makes grant funding available to local units of
government to assist with the purchase of fire equipment for all firefighters, fire gear for
firefighters that are on-call, part-time, or volunteer. A local government includes townships and
an authority established by a township thru resolution, motion, or charter.

Grant Program applications must be submitted by the chief administrative officer with a proposed
budget for how the funding will be used. Grant awards offer an award of up to $50,000 with
applications due September 30, 2026.

Funding is prioritized for local governments that directly employ firefighters and have direct
authority, supervision or operational control of firefighters. Award amounts will be prorated to
ensure all local governments within this priority group receive funding. If additional funds
remain, other local governments may be considered. However, based on anticipated demand,
Treasury does not expect funding to be available beyond the priority group.

As a one-time 2026 appropriation, the Michigan Department of Treasury was appropriated $8
million to award fire equipment and fire gear grants to qualified local governments. At least
$4 million must be distributed to local governments with predominantly on-call, part-time, or
volunteer fire departments.

BAFA has indicated that, if the Township receives the grant, all funds would be used for turnout
gear set (pant and coat).
